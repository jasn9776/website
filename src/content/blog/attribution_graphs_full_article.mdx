## Epistemic status

Pre-registered on 16 September 2026, before any data was collected, in an append-only log with 40+
dated deviation entries. **Fifteen predictions were registered across three stages; eight failed.**
All fifteen are reported below against their stated thresholds.

| stage | predictions | failed |
|---|---|---|
| Main corpus, Gemma-2-2B (255 graphs) | P1–P5 | 3 |
| Cross-dictionary arms (Llama, matched frames) | C0–C4 | 3 |
| Category replication (Llama, 255 graphs) | D1–D5 | 2 |

What I am confident in: the measurements. Roughly 700 attributions in all — two full 255-prompt
corpora with **zero failures in either**, plus three 66-graph matched-frame arms — with metrics
validated against the library's own implementation to 1.1×10⁻⁶, two attribution backends agreeing to
1×10⁻⁴ on the same prompts, and a controlled manipulation whose direction appears in every arm.

What the reader should discount: anything resting on the 40 human ratings. One rater, not blind to
the hypotheses, a median of 262 seconds per graph against the hours the original work describes, and
criteria developed in discussion with an LLM assistant — which also rated, so agreement between
those streams is not independent convergence. **Those ratings exist only for Gemma-2-2B with
per-layer transcoders**, and nothing in the cross-model work touches them.

Three claims are corrected inside this document rather than quietly removed: an estimate of the
direct-path share that was wrong by fourfold, a proposed mechanism for the length effect that my own
later data refutes, and a superlative about induction that turns out to be partly an artefact of
prompt length.

## What I found

**There is no usable pre-flight check for attribution-graph quality on Gemma-2-2B.** That search was
pre-registered and the negative result was the original headline.

**The more useful finding is that almost none of it transfers.** Running the same corpus on a second
model, and the same prompts under a cross-layer dictionary, moves nearly every headline number:

| claim | Gemma-2-2B | Llama-3.2-1B | |
|---|---|---|---|
| replacement's between-category range | 0.036 | **0.142** | 3.9× |
| category eta² on replacement | 0.238 | **0.626** | 2.6× |
| induction's rank by replacement (of 8) | 1st | **5th** | inverted |
| length effect, within-pair slope | +0.0458 | **+0.0087** | 5× weaker |
| predicted-vs-observed category ranking | +0.071 | **+0.571** | underpowered, n = 8 |

So the central claim of the original analysis — that replacement score is *statistically structured
but practically flat*, and therefore useless for choosing prompts — is **a property of Gemma-2-2B
with GemmaScope transcoders, not of the method.** On Llama-3.2-1B the metric spans 0.142 across
prompt types, over half the published gap between dictionary families, and category explains 63% of
its variance. A practitioner who read "the metric is flat, don't triage on it" would be wrong there.

**What does generalise is thinner, and it is mostly about directions rather than sizes:**

- **Replacement score rises with prompt length**, in every arm tested, with content held fixed. The
  magnitude varies 3.5× between dictionaries and the direction never flips.
- **It is not explained by feature count.** Across three dictionaries at a fixed +4.78-token
  manipulation, normalising the effect by features added makes the spread *worse* (3.51× → 6.31×),
  and the dictionary with the *fewest* features per token has the *strongest* effect. An earlier
  estimate that about half the effect was feature count does not survive this.
- **Coverage and insight come apart**, and the robust example is not the one I first reported.
  Known/unknown entity prompts top the length-adjusted ranking on **both** models and were rated 0 in
  four of six cases, with no graph ever rated 2.
- **Completeness is less discriminative than replacement** on both models (P4 and D5).
- **The matched-frame design cannot separate tokens from features**: `r(d_tok, d_feat)` is 0.9994 to
  0.9998 in every arm.

And one measurement result worth as much as any finding: **two attribution backends agree to 1×10⁻⁴**
on mean replacement over the same prompts, so none of the above is a library artefact.

The corpus, 510 graphs with per-prompt metrics, the ratings with their written reasoning, and the
pre-registration with its full deviation log are all released.

## The problem

Circuit tracing via attribution graphs is the leading method for working out how a language model
produced a particular output. Two things are true of it at once.

It produces genuinely new findings — multi-step reasoning, forward planning in poetry, a model with a
hidden goal. And its authors report that it gave satisfying insight on roughly a quarter of the
prompts they tried, at a cost of a few hours of human effort per graph.

So: a high failure rate, expensive attempts, and no way to tell in advance which bucket you are in. A
researcher picks a prompt, spends an afternoon, and three times in four comes away without a
mechanism they can state.

The tooling already computes two quality scores for every graph. Replacement score measures the
fraction of end-to-end influence from input tokens to output that flows through interpretable
features rather than through error nodes. Completeness is a softer, per-node version. The methods
paper notes these are useful "to flag prompts our method performs poorly on" — then never develops
that use. They are reported as dataset-wide averages for comparing replacement models, never as a
per-prompt instrument, and nobody had tested whether they predict a human's success at reading a
particular graph.

**Two questions, and a third that the first two forced:**

- Do cheap prompt-side properties — token count, next-token entropy, top-1 probability, token
  frequency, prompt category — predict attribution-graph quality?
- Does replacement score track whether a human can actually extract a mechanism from the graph?
- **Does any answer to those questions survive a change of model or dictionary?**

---

## What replacement score measures

An attribution graph is built on a local replacement model: each MLP is swapped for a transcoder,
which re-expresses that MLP's output as a sparse sum of interpretable features; whatever the
transcoder fails to reconstruct is injected as an error node; and attention patterns and
normalisation denominators are frozen at the values they took on this prompt. What remains is linear,
so contributions add and every edge carries an exact coefficient.

The graph has four kinds of node, two of them asymmetric in a way that drives everything below:

| Node | Incoming | Outgoing |
|---|---|---|
| feature | yes | yes |
| error | **none** | yes |
| token embedding | **none** | yes |
| logit | yes | none |

Influence is pushed backwards from the logits one edge at a time. The graph is a DAG, so the series
terminates — and it can only terminate at a node with no inputs. There are exactly two such kinds.
Hence **replacement score is the embedding share**, and its complement is the error share: across the
corpus, replacement ≈ 1 − total error mass to three decimal places.

One consequence often reached for: a token's embedding enters the residual stream at layer 0 and is
never removed, so the unembedding can read it at layer 25 with no feature in between, and that mass
counts toward replacement while explaining nothing. I measured that path exactly and it is **9.5%** of
the score — real, but small, and progressively diluted by the normalisation denominators. It is *not*
why the metric misbehaves; see the correction below.

What matters is the framing. Replacement score measures **coverage** — can this computation be
expressed in features — and coverage is easiest to achieve when there is little computation to cover.

## The corpus

255 prompts, 8 categories, each grounded in a limitation the methods paper documents qualitatively
but never quantifies.

| Category | n | Grounded in | Validated by (Gemma) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Obfuscated text (4 corruption rungs) | 44 | reconstruction-error blind spot | 11 complete ladders; entropy non-monotonic, reported |
| Factual recall | 40 | published success case | answers 89% with a `Fact:` prefix on short frames |
| Known vs unknown entity | 32 | inactive-feature / suppression blind spot | round birth years 5/16 vs 16/16, Fisher p = 6.8×10⁻⁵ |
| Multi-hop | 32 | longer chains, error accumulation | answers 94%; own-state-capital cities removed |
| Code completion | 29 | untested territory | — |
| Two-digit arithmetic | 28 | published success case | correct 96% |
| Syntactic agreement | 26 | short mechanical control | 18/18 by verb-logit margin, no attraction errors |
| Induction / copying | 24 | attention blind spot (QK frozen) | 24/24 strict three-character copying |

Every category claim was checked before any graph was attributed, and two categories were rebuilt
when the check failed. Sentence-frame induction prompts turned out to be solved by frame completion —
the model predicted `ran` or `was` in 20 of 20 — so the category was rebuilt on bigram repetition.

A ninth category, multiple choice, was dropped after two designs failed. Gemma-2-2B does not bind
answers to option letters: the arrow format continued the option list (12/12 predicted `c`, not an
option) and the answer format defaulted to `a` 75% of the time. A redesign as forced choice between
two in-context words failed differently — 79% of choices went to the first-listed option, because
repeating the stem created an induction pattern and in-context copying overrode factual knowledge.
Per a rule fixed before the check, it was dropped rather than redesigned a third time.

Prompt length was varied deliberately within category so it is not confounded between categories
(eta² = 0.131). That decision turned out to matter more than expected.

**The category validations are Gemma results**, and that matters for everything in the replication
section. Re-run on Llama-3.2-1B: induction, arithmetic, multi-hop and known/unknown entity all pass;
the entity check replicates more cleanly than on Gemma (round years 3/16 known vs 15/16 unknown,
Fisher p = 3.9×10⁻⁵). Factual recall fails a pooled 0.70 answer-rate gate at 0.675, but the failure
is entirely one frame format — `capital_short` 12/12, `capital_long` 11/12, `sport_short` 3/4,
**`capital_med` 1/12** — so restricted to short and long frames it is 26/28 = 0.93. Code completion,
obfuscated text and syntactic agreement have **no validity evidence on Llama at all**: their expected
answers are undefined, and the Gemma-specific checks (verb-logit margins, the entropy ladder) were
not reproduced.

## The five pre-registered results, on Gemma-2-2B

| | Prediction | Threshold | Observed | |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **P1** | category ranking predictable from documented failure modes | Spearman, 8 categories | +0.071 (p = 0.87) | **FAILS** |
| **P2** | replacement does not discriminate between categories | range < 0.15 **and** eta² < 0.20 | range 0.036 ✓, eta² 0.238 ✗ | **FAILS** |
| **P3** | error location discriminates where error quantity does not | ≥ 2× | 1.86× | **FAILS** |
| **P4** | completeness even less discriminative than replacement | smaller range | 0.008 vs 0.036 | **HOLDS** |
| **P5** | replacement vs human rating weakly positive | \|rho\| < 0.4 | +0.243 (p = 0.13) | **HOLDS** |

**P5 holds, but its threshold was nearly vacuous and I should say so.** The prediction's text was
"positive but weak"; the registered threshold was \|rho\| < 0.4, which an uncorrelated or negative
result would also satisfy. And +0.243 at p = 0.13 does not establish the positive direction. So P5
establishes only that replacement score is not *strongly* related to human readability. Together with
the two falsification rules discussed later, this is the third registered criterion that tested a
bound rather than the quantity the project cared about — the main methodological lesson here is that
a threshold written as \|x\| < k, with no direction and no effect-size floor, can be passed by a
result that means nothing.

**P2 split.** The range was four times better than it had to be — replacement discriminated even
*less* than predicted, which is the direction the prediction wanted — but eta² came in over its own
0.20 ceiling. The two disagree because eta² is a ratio and within-category spread is smaller still
(standard deviations 0.008 to 0.026), so entity graphs sit inside 0.024 of each other. Category
accounts for a quarter of the variance in a quantity that barely varies. For scale, the published gap
between per-layer and cross-layer dictionaries is 0.37 vs 0.61 — seven times the entire
between-category spread. **On Gemma, the metric distinguished methods, not prompts.** The replication
section is where that sentence acquires its caveat.

**P1 failed informatively.** The predicted ordering was derived from the failure modes the methods
paper documents — frozen QK for induction, reconstruction error for obfuscated text, suppression for
unknown entities. Those limitations predicted the metric's own ordering at rho = 0.07. The two
largest errors ran in opposite directions: induction was predicted 7th of 8 and ranked 1st;
arithmetic was predicted 3rd and ranked last.

**P3 was the narrowest miss** — 44.4% against 23.8%, a ratio of 1.86 against a threshold of 2.0 — and
the *mechanism* proposed for it was wrong in two of three cases. Obfuscated text was predicted to
concentrate error on corrupted tokens and is third-lowest in concentration; induction was predicted
to concentrate at the copy source and is least concentrated of all. The most concentrated categories
are entity and multi-hop, both dominated by error at the read-out position.

## Replacement score responds to prompt length

Not predicted, and the most robust result in the project.

Capital-city and state-capital facts were posed in short, medium and long frames — same fact, same
answer, same model, only the wording around it changing. **27 facts appear in both a short and a long
frame**, 12 of those also in a medium frame. Across the 27 matched short/long pairs, replacement rose
with frame length in **27 of 27** cases: mean +0.0323, median +0.020, Wilcoxon statistic 0 (not a
single reversal), p = 1.5×10⁻⁸. Pairing is at the level of the fact.

On the 12 items posed in all three frames the increase is monotonic — 0.7147 → 0.7378 → 0.7703,
Friedman p = 6.1×10⁻⁶.

Two things made this damaging for the triage use *on Gemma*. The effect is as large as the entire
between-category range: padding a prompt moves the same fact by 0.032 and the eight categories span
0.036. And it is a controlled manipulation, not a correlation across prompts — the fact, the answer
and the model are identical. The matched pairs also give a **larger** estimate than the
cross-sectional regression: **+0.0458 logit/token** within a fact against +0.0133 across prompts,
3.4× bigger, with R² 0.81. The controlled number is the one to quote.

**A note on units.** Every regression coefficient here is in **logit points per token** — +0.0133
(simple regression, all 255), +0.018 (the four-predictor model), +0.0176 (fitted on the 211
non-obfuscated prompts as a length-only null), +0.0458 (within matched pairs). All on the same scale,
which is what makes those comparisons meaningful. The **raw mean differences** — +0.032 across pairs,
the 0.036 between-category range, the frame means — are in **probability units**. At p ≈ 0.72 one
logit point is worth about 0.2 in probability.

### The `Fact:` prefix is not driving it

Short frames carry a `Fact:` prefix and long frames do not, so the headline contrast changed format
as well as length.

The obvious control does not work, and the reason is worth stating because it is easy to propose.
Re-attributing the short frames *without* the prefix would not isolate format: the prefix is what
makes those frames elicit an answer at all — `capital_short` answers 0.33 without it against 0.92
with it. Stripping it compares graphs that explain a continuation against graphs that explain an
answer, confounding format with task success. The experiment would produce a number and settle
nothing.

The control that works was already in the corpus, and the prefix distribution was checked rather than
assumed: **0 of 12 medium frames and 0 of 27 long frames carry it**, against 26 of 33 short frames
(the 7 `city_language` short prompts never had it). So **medium → long changes length with no change
of format**.

- medium → long: **+0.0325** raw over 5 added tokens, 12/12 positive, Wilcoxon p = 4.9×10⁻⁴,
  **0.0065 per token**
- short → long: +0.0323 over 4.78 tokens, 27/27, **0.0068 per token**

Decomposing the ladder within the 12 three-frame items, the per-token effect is not constant:
short → med is +0.0232 over 2 tokens (0.0116 per token, the leg where format changes) and med → long
is +0.0325 over 5 tokens (0.0065 per token, format constant). Taking the clean leg as the length
rate, length alone predicts 0.0455 of the 0.0557 full span — **roughly 82% length, about 18% format
or nonlinearity in length**. At n = 12 with a two-token denominator on the first leg, that 18% is
indicative rather than estimated.

### The corruption ladder is length too

*Exploratory; no per-rung test on replacement was registered.*

The obfuscation category is the corpus's one dose-response design: 11 base sentences at 4 corruption
rungs, every comparison within the same sentence. Raw, corruption appears to raise replacement —
clean → character-substitution is +0.018, positive in 9 of 11 ladders, Friedman p = 0.018, Wilcoxon
p = 0.0068.

It is length. Corruption fragments text into character-level tokens, so the prompts gain 10.4 tokens
over that contrast. A length coefficient fitted on the 211 **non**-obfuscated prompts (+0.0176
logit/token, p = 3×10⁻¹³) predicts +0.035 — twice what happened. Every rung comes in below its
length-only prediction, monotonically:

| Rung | Tokens | Observed | Predicted from length | Residual |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| clean | 5.6 | 0.7223 | 0.7223 | — |
| typo | 6.8 | 0.7176 | 0.7264 | −0.0088 |
| random caps | 11.0 | 0.7291 | 0.7408 | −0.0117 |
| char substitution | 16.0 | 0.7403 | 0.7574 | −0.0171 |

Within-ladder fixed effects agree: the rung coefficient flips from +0.0330 (p = 6×10⁻⁵) to −0.0229
once token count enters, while length takes +0.0158 (p = 0.013). So corruption does not raise
replacement; the apparent rise is fragmentation into more tokens, and conditional on length
corruption slightly lowers it. How much is not identified — r(rung, tokens) = 0.897, n = 11.

A designed manipulation entirely absorbed by its own side-effect on token count is the sharpest
illustration of the length effect in the corpus.

## What the length effect acts through: not feature count

My first guess was structural: each additional token adds an embedding node, a terminal for backward
influence, so a longer prompt should collect more mass on embeddings whether or not any feature was
involved. Splitting the score by path length on all 255 graphs says otherwise. Across the same 27
pairs the **direct** share *falls* slightly with length (−0.009, positive in 11 of 27) while
**mediated** replacement rises in **26 of 27** (+0.035, p = 3.0×10⁻⁸). So padding raises the
feature-mediated part, not the residual-stream part.

That leaves a second structural reading, one node type over: feature count also scales with token
positions, so a longer prompt has more features to pass through — more *routes*, not more
*computation*.

**Within the corpus, that reading looked about half right.** Replacement rises +0.0133 per token
unconditionally and +0.0068 holding total feature count fixed (p = 0.035), while holding length fixed
each additional feature also raises it (p = 0.015). The two correlate at 0.83 and jointly explain
almost nothing more than either alone — R² 0.195 against 0.176 and 0.181. Feature *density* carries
independent signal (p = 0.006) and varies 583–1,649 across the corpus.

**The matched-frame design cannot adjudicate this, and the reason is instructive.** Lengthening a
frame adds 4.78 tokens and 4,442 features — about 925 per token, the corpus mean density. Tokens and
features move in lockstep within pairs (**r = 0.999**), and a model containing both is pure
collinearity: the token coefficient flips sign and inflates eightfold, from +0.046 to −0.380. That is
an artefact, recorded here precisely so nobody quotes it.

### Three dictionaries refute it

Running the same prompts under different dictionaries supplies what the within-corpus design could
not: the token delta is pinned at **+4.78 in every arm** while feature density varies threefold.

| arm | dictionary | features / added token | per-token effect | per-feature effect | mean path |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gemma-2-2B | GemmaScope per-layer | 929.8 | 0.00677 | 7.28×10⁻⁶ | 3.152 |
| Llama-3.2-1B | per-layer | 544.5 | 0.00196 | 3.59×10⁻⁶ | 3.453 |
| Llama-3.2-1B | **cross-layer** | **302.8** | **0.00687** | 2.27×10⁻⁵ | **2.760** |

If the length effect were feature count, normalising each arm by features added should make the arms
agree. **It does the opposite: the spread is 3.51× per token and 6.31× per feature.** Density has no
monotonic relation to the effect either — the lowest-density arm has the strongest effect and the
middle arm the weakest.

**The within-corpus numbers stand as facts. Their mechanistic reading is withdrawn.** The "about
half" estimate came from a between-prompt regression inside one dictionary plus a two-arm comparison
in which density and effect happened to co-vary; the third arm breaks it.

What *does* order with the effect, across all three arms and within Llama alone, is **mean path
length** — shorter paths, stronger effect. One story is that influence reaching embeddings in fewer
hops lets added tokens contribute terminal mass more directly instead of dissipating into error
nodes. But n = 3, the correlate was chosen after seeing the data, and one point is a different model.
So it is registered rather than reported:

> **C5, registered before any Gemma cross-layer arm is run.** If path length rather than feature
> density drives the length effect, a Gemma cross-layer arm should show mean path length below 3.152
> **and** a per-token effect at or above 0.00677. Short paths with a weak effect would make the
> path-length ordering a coincidence on three points.

## The cross-dictionary arms: C0–C4

*Registered before any transcoder was loaded. Same 27 matched facts, same +4.78-token manipulation.*

| | Prediction | Threshold | Observed | |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **C0** | the length effect replicates on a second model with per-layer transcoders | ≥ 24 of 27 | 21/27 | **FAILS** |
| **C1** | it survives the cross-layer transcoder | ≥ 24 of 27 | **27/27** | **HOLDS** |
| **C2** | the cross-layer slope is *smaller*, same model | CLT < PLT | +0.0281 vs +0.0087, **3.2× larger** | **FAILS, backwards** |
| **C3** | cross-layer replacement is higher | CLT > PLT | 0.7133 vs 0.7366, lower | **FAILS** |
| **C4** | cross-layer path length is shorter | CLT < PLT | 2.7597 vs 3.4533 | **HOLDS** |

**C0.** The direction holds (Wilcoxon p = 1×10⁻⁴) but little else: the slope is +0.0087 logit/token
against Gemma's +0.0458, with R² 0.148 against 0.807. On the 22 pairs the model answered in both
frames the slope loses significance entirely (+0.0077, p = 0.152). So what remains of the effect on
Llama's per-layer dictionary is concentrated in prompts where the model did something other than the
task. **Both backends agree on this**, so it is not a library artefact.

**C1 and C2.** The cross-layer transcoder gives 27/27 and survives the answered-only restriction
(22/22, p = 8×10⁻⁴) where the per-layer arm does not. Its slope is 3.2× the per-layer arm's on the
same model and backend, where I predicted smaller — it rescues the effect from failing to holding.
Reproduced on an independent re-run: slope +0.0280 → +0.0281, replacement 0.7134 → 0.7133, path
length identical.

**C3 and C4.** The published per-layer/cross-layer replacement gap (0.61 vs 0.37) does **not**
reproduce — cross-layer replacement is slightly lower here. The path-length difference does (2.3 vs
3.7 published; 2.76 vs 3.45 observed). C4 holding while C3 fails is reassuring about the setup rather
than worrying: the dictionary behaves as advertised structurally even though the headline metric does
not move as advertised.

**Backend equivalence, measured.** Cross-layer attribution works only on the TransformerLens backend
— the nnsight backend raises `MissedProviderError` on cross-layer decoders, because such a decoder
writes into several downstream layers and that backend's hook injection misses a module. So a
same-backend per-layer baseline was run and the two backends compared directly: **mean replacement
0.7365 vs 0.7366**, mean path length 3.4500 vs 3.4533 — inside the 3.4×10⁻⁴ re-attribution ceiling.

**A Gemma cross-layer arm is not feasible on free compute**, and the reason is structural rather than
a tuning failure. TransformerLens holds cross-layer decoders in CPU RAM and streams them, so the
426k Gemma dictionary needs 28.5 GB of transcoders plus 5.2 GB of bf16 model = **33.7 GB against
31.5 GB available**. `lazy_decoder=True` is already the default. The only backend that supports
cross-layer transcoders is the one that cannot fit Gemma's in RAM. It needs ~48 GB of RAM; the GPU is
not the constraint — the 20.4 GB Llama cross-layer set peaked at **7.0 GB** of VRAM. **So the
dictionary question is answered on Llama-3.2-1B only**, which matters because C0 shows the effect is
strongly model-dependent.

## Coverage without insight

This is the project's qualitative finding, and the version I first reported needs correcting.

### What I claimed

Induction had the highest mean replacement score of the eight categories, at 0.7455, and **every
rated induction graph scored 0**. An induction prompt is `snerrik yolvantz snerrik yolvantz snerrik`;
the model predicts `yol` at p = 0.95; the graph traces it cleanly to two raw embeddings at the
earlier occurrences with almost nothing lost to error. Coverage is excellent and the explanation is
*it predicted `yol` because `yol` appeared earlier* — which you knew from reading the prompt.

The mechanism you actually want — how the model knew to look at *that* token — is the induction
head's query-key computation, frozen into a constant before attribution began. It is not in these
graphs and no amount of looking at them will find it. Anthropic say as much about their own version
of this case, calling it arguably a regression against earlier methods that could at least identify
induction heads.

**That is a statement about this method as configured, not about attribution graphs in general.**
Attention is frozen because the local replacement model needs linearity; approaches that attribute
*through* attention — QK attribution, and replacement models that decompose attention heads rather
than freezing them — target exactly this failure.

### Two corrections

**The superlative is partly prompt length.** Induction prompts are the longest category on both
models — 13.1 tokens on Gemma and 15.0 on Llama, against 8.4–12.1 for everything else. Partialling
out token count demotes Gemma's induction from **rank 1 to rank 3** of eight. So "the highest mean
replacement score of the eight categories" is true raw and misleading as a mechanism claim: part of
the lead is the length effect this project itself discovered.

**And it does not replicate.** On Llama-3.2-1B induction ranks **5th** of eight, raw and
length-adjusted alike. Induction passed its manipulation check on that model (it does copy nonce
bigrams), so this is a real test, not a broken category. The inversion's replacement-score half is
Gemma-specific.

### The robust example is entity, not induction

| | Gemma, length-adjusted | Llama, length-adjusted | human ratings (0/1/2) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **known/unknown entity** | **1st** (+0.0674) | **1st** (+0.1945) | **4 / 2 / 0** |
| induction | 3rd (+0.0294) | 5th (+0.0915) | 5 / 0 / 0 |

Known/unknown entity tops the length-adjusted ranking on **both** models, has the lowest total error
mass on Llama (0.254), and was rated 0 in four of six cases with **no graph ever rated 2**. Known and
unknown entities are also indistinguishable by replacement score (0.742 vs 0.745), and the reason is
the same in both: the graph shows the model *formatting* an answer without showing it *retrieving*
one, whether or not the person is real.

That is a cleaner demonstration of coverage-without-insight than induction, because it survives
length adjustment and replicates across models. Induction remains the more vivid illustration — 5/5
zeros, and a crisp mechanical explanation in frozen QK — but it is Gemma-specific and partly length,
and should be presented that way.

The full rating picture across the 40 rated Gemma graphs:

| Category | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| factual recall | 0 | 1 | 4 |
| multi-hop | 1 | 2 | 2 |
| code completion | 0 | 3 | 0 |
| syntactic agreement | 1 | 3 | 0 |
| obfuscated | 2 | 3 | 0 |
| entity known/unknown | 4 | 2 | 0 |
| arithmetic | 4 | 3 | 0 |
| induction | 5 | 0 | 0 |

All six top ratings are geographic factual recall or multi-hop — entity → country or state → answer,
the shape of the published Dallas→Austin circuit. Nothing else produced one. Code completion produced
no 0s and no 2s, every graph a 1, because the feature labels available for code tokens were too
implausible to name an intermediate — a property of the feature descriptions, not of the graphs.

## Does the category picture generalise? D1–D5

*Registered before the Llama corpus run. 255 graphs, per-layer transcoders, 0 failures.*

| | Prediction | Threshold | Llama | Gemma | |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **D1** | range stays small | < 0.15 | **0.1418** | 0.0363 | HOLDS, marginally |
| **D2** | category still explains > 1/5 of variance | eta² ≥ 0.20 | **0.6255** | 0.2383 | HOLDS |
| **D3** | the induction inversion replicates | top 3 of 8 | **rank 5** | rank 1 | **FAILS** |
| **D4** | documented failure modes still fail to predict the ranking | \|Spearman\| < 0.4 | **+0.571** (p = 0.139) | +0.071 | **FAILS** |
| **D5** | completeness still flatter than replacement | smaller range | 0.0303 vs 0.1418 | 0.008 vs 0.036 | HOLDS |

**D1 holds on the letter and fails on the spirit.** 0.1418 against a 0.15 ceiling is a 5% margin, and
the range is 3.9× Gemma's. Combined with eta² of 0.626 — category explaining 63% of variance against
24% — the claim the original analysis rested on, *statistically structured but practically flat*, does
not hold on Llama. A 0.14 spread across prompt types is over half the published gap between
dictionary families. **On this model replacement score does discriminate between prompt types in a
way that could plausibly be acted on.**

One category carries about half of that. `code_completion` sits at 0.6038 against Gemma's 0.7357,
with the highest total error mass of any category (0.396) — plausible for code under a 1B base model
and its per-layer dictionary. Excluding it, the range is **0.0627** and eta² **0.3972** — still 1.7×
Gemma on both measures, so the finding survives the outlier but is much less dramatic. Both numbers
are reported because they support different sentences.

**D4's +0.571 is not established.** At n = 8, p = 0.139 — neither +0.571 nor Gemma's +0.071 is
distinguishable from zero, nor from each other. The point estimate is much higher and the test is
underpowered; that is the whole claim.

**The two models' category rankings agree at Spearman +0.476** (p = 0.233) raw, and about +0.50
length-adjusted. Moderate, not significant at n = 8, and unchanged by length adjustment — so length
is not what makes the models disagree.

A secondary pass with task failures removed (234 of 255 prompts) changes almost nothing: eta² 0.6228
against 0.6255, identical range, only factual recall and multi-hop moving rank. None of this rides on
prompts where the model failed its task.

**A registered rule I did not apply, and it may change D4's verdict.** The pre-registration fixed a
validity gate before the run: a category whose answer rate falls below 0.70 is *excluded* from D3 and
D4. `factual_recall` came in at 0.675 pooled, and the table above includes it. The failure is one
frame format rather than the category — `capital_med` answers 1 of 12 against 26 of 28 for short and
long frames — which is an argument for the gate having been written at the wrong granularity, not for
ignoring it. D3 is unaffected: dropping the category leaves induction at rank 4 of 7, still outside
the top 3. **D4 is not.** `factual_recall` is observed rank 2 and was predicted high, so removing a
concordant pair should pull rho down, possibly under the 0.4 threshold — which would flip D4 from
FAILS to HOLDS. That recomputation is owed and has not been run, so D4 is reported as computed with
this attached.

The same rule says nothing about a category with **no defined expected answer**, and three of the
eight are in that position on Llama: `code_completion`, `obfuscated` and `syntactic_agreement` have no
validity evidence on this model at all. All three sit inside the rankings being tested,
`code_completion` drives half the range, and `syntactic_agreement` ranks 4th with nothing validating
it. They were included by default because the gate did not cover them.

And the length adjustment is a single global linear fit on token count pooled across categories, so
the slope it removes is a between-prompt slope that itself contains between-category length
differences. Crude; read the adjusted ranks as "length explains some of this", not as calibrated
effects.

## How much of the metric bypasses the features — a correction

A token's embedding enters the residual stream at layer 0 and is never removed, so the unembedding
can read it at layer 25 with no feature in between. I first estimated that path's size by parsing the
logit's displayed top edges on the 40 rated graphs. Recomputing it exactly — from the full influence
vector, splitting replacement by path length, no thresholding and no top-k — gives a much smaller
number:

| | parsed from displayed edges | computed exactly |
|---|---|---|
| mean direct share | 0.372 | **0.095** (median 0.091, range 0.023–0.184) |
| correlation with replacement | +0.556 | +0.157 Pearson, +0.124 Spearman |
| correlation with human rating | +0.033 | −0.089 |

**So the claim that much of the metric bypasses the features is wrong. About a tenth of it does.**
The parsed estimate was inflated roughly fourfold, because the direct edge is nearly always among a
logit's top few inputs while the thousands of small feature paths carrying most of the mass never
appear in a truncated view. The correction is left visible rather than quietly swapped. **The general
lesson: any quantity that sums edge weights must come from the adjacency matrix, because a display
threshold is not a random sample of edges.**

Splitting replacement into direct and mediated parts:

| | Spearman with human rating | mean by rating 0 / 1 / 2 |
|---|---|---|
| replacement score | +0.243 (p = 0.13) | 0.731 / 0.729 / 0.755 |
| mediated replacement | +0.297 (p = 0.06) | 0.656 / 0.664 / 0.672 |
| direct share | −0.089 (p = 0.59) | 0.103 / 0.089 / 0.110 |

Removing the direct component leaves the correlation essentially unchanged — Δρ = 0.054 on n = 40,
both p > 0.05 — but monotonic, where raw replacement dips at rating 1. I would not lean on either
half. Mediated replacement is close to an affine rescaling of the total, so little reranking is
arithmetically possible; the monotonic trend is three steps of 0.008 against within-category standard
deviations of 0.008–0.026, with only **6 graphs** in the top rating bin. Mediated's spread across
ratings (0.016) is also *smaller* than raw's (0.024): monotonic and less discriminative at once.

At category level the reshuffle is larger — code completion rises from 5th to 1st, induction falls
from 1st to 6th — but that is reshuffling means spanning 0.036 using a component that varies more
between graphs than the categories differ. **P1 re-scored on mediated replacement gives Spearman
−0.524** (p = 0.18, n = 8): once the interpretable part is isolated, the documented failure modes
predict the ordering substantially backwards. Exploratory, not a re-test, and eight categories is a
small n.

## So does a triage rule exist?

No — and there are now two independent arguments, neither of which is the one I registered.

### The registered rules did not fire

Section 5 set out two routes to a null. The first: category explains under 10% of variance in every
outcome **and** nothing survives multiple-comparison correction. Category explains 21% to 47%, and 47
of 66 tested terms survive Benjamini–Hochberg. The second: leave-one-category-out R² at or below zero
for **all** outcomes. Three of six are positive. My analysis script evaluated both and printed
`NULL RESULT HEADLINE: False`.

For clarity about the 47: six outcomes were each tested against eleven terms — seven category dummies
comparing one category against arithmetic as reference, and the four cheap continuous predictors. So
66 questions of the form "does A differ from B" or "does X move with Y", and 47 came back yes. The
count is partly an artefact of the reference choice, since arithmetic is the lowest category on the
primary outcome and contrasts against an extreme are easier to detect; and the six outcomes are
strongly correlated, so the effective number of independent tests is well below 66.

**The rules were badly specified.** Both asked whether *any* signal exists. The project's question is
whether the signal is large enough and portable enough to decide where to spend an afternoon — which
Section 6.1 of the same document had already insisted on. With 255 graphs and a noise floor of
5×10⁻⁵, "nothing reaches significance" was a bar only a broken instrument could clear. What I should
have written was a threshold on the quantities that matter: a null if the best out-of-category R²
falls below about 0.15, or if the between-category range of the primary outcome falls below 0.05. Both
would have fired. I am recording that the criteria I registered tested the wrong thing, not
retrofitting better ones.

### Argument one: the structure does not transfer across prompt types

| Outcome | Category eta² | Leave-one-category-out R² |
|---|---|---|
| final-position error share | 0.431 | **+0.275** |
| replacement score (logit) | 0.238 | +0.067 |
| total error mass | 0.242 | +0.053 |
| completeness (logit) | 0.210 | −0.143 |
| error concentration (Gini) | 0.444 | −0.186 |
| mean path length | 0.470 | −0.535 |

**The ordering inverts.** The outcomes with the most in-sample category structure generalise worst.
The one that transfers appreciably — final-position error share — correlates **−0.691 with token
count**, and length alone explains 47.7% of its variance, more than the fitted model recovers out of
category. The share is a ratio over positions, so a shorter prompt concentrates error at the read-out
position for arithmetic rather than mechanistic reasons. **The only rule that generalises is prompt
length in disguise.**

Cheap continuous predictors beat the category label in-sample — R² 0.299 against 0.238, driven by
token count (+0.018 logit/token) and a token-frequency proxy (−0.033), both at p < 10⁻⁴. That is the
practically relevant model, since a practitioner has a prompt and not a category. Out of category it
drops fourfold to **0.067**. A model combining both is uninformative for inference: maximum VIF 23.1
against a pre-set threshold of 5, because entropy and top-1 probability are near-collinear at
r = −0.958. Those two reach nothing individually (p = 0.10 and p = 0.13), but at that correlation
each t-test asks only whether one adds anything given the other, so their joint contribution is
**undetermined** rather than absent. A joint test was not run.

### Argument two: it does not transfer across models either

This one is new, and it is the stronger of the two.

Even if a practitioner calibrated a rule on one model, the quantities it would rest on move when the
model or the dictionary changes: the between-category range by 3.9×, category eta² by 2.6×, the
length slope by 3.5×, and the ranking of prompt types enough to move induction from 1st to 5th. A
per-prompt instrument whose behaviour is a property of the model-and-dictionary pairing cannot be
used as a general instrument — it would have to be re-validated per setup, which costs more than the
tracing it was meant to triage.

**That is a better argument against per-prompt triage than the original null**, because it does not
depend on the Gemma effects being small. It would hold even if they were large.

## The human rating, and what it cost to do honestly

40 graphs drawn from eight quantile bins of replacement score, 10 re-inserted as unmarked duplicates,
50 presentations in randomised order, rated blind to every metric. Three-point scale: **2** for a
causal path statable in one sentence naming the intermediate features, **1** for a meaningful feature
but no path, **0** for neither.

Criteria were developed on practice graphs from outside the rated sample and frozen in a file
committed before rating began. Two mechanical checks before awarding a 2: the trace must report at
least one cross-position edge, and a named feature must sit at a non-final token position. A crossing
without an intermediate is routing, not computation; an intermediate without a crossing is the
read-out token elaborating itself.

**What the rater saw.** Not the library's pruned viewer — the pre-registration said that and D3.11
corrected it before rating began. Each presentation was the backward-reachable subgraph from the
single top-1 logit, selected by recursively taking each node's top four incoming edges to depth four,
with a weight floor that only trims beyond the top three. Three properties bear on the results. It is
**edge-weight selection, not influence-based pruning** — no node threshold was applied, so the
picture is not "the most influential nodes". Because the top three inputs always survive, **the
diagram can display a complete path carrying almost no influence**; the rubric handles this by
excluding magnitude from the rating and flagging weak chains in the written reason, but "the graph
showed a path" is partly a property of the viewer. And depth four against a corpus mean path length
of 3.7 means **longer graphs were truncated more often** — and path length is the outcome with the
highest category eta² (0.470), so that truncation is unevenly distributed across categories.

**Reliability.** Intra-rater quadratic weighted kappa on the duplicates: **0.889**, 9 of 10 pairs
identical. The single disagreement is traceable — the same graph rated 0 at presentation 20 and 1 at
presentation 45, spanning a criterion articulated mid-run and recorded at the time.

Two further streams, both exploratory (registered as D3.11 before any rating). An LLM rated the same
50 from the stored text with the same written rubric (kappa with me: **0.68**); a second LLM audited
my stated reasoning against each graph. Neither is independent evidence: both were given criteria I
wrote, and one helped write them. Agreement measures whether the criteria are **articulable**, not
whether they are **shared**. Both ran only after all 50 human ratings were recorded, in batches of
ten pasted into fresh chats, with **no duplicate pair sharing a batch**. Presentations within a batch
share context, so LLM ratings are not independent across presentations; reported, not corrected.

**The audit is the part I would keep.** Giving a judge the graph, the rubric, my grade and my written
reason, and asking *separately* whether the reason follows from the graph and whether the grade
follows from the reason, produced a number rating studies rarely report: **12% of ratings were
defensible but rested on an unsupported reason.** The judge disagreed outright on **30%**.

And the disagreements were not scattered. **Five of the eight flagged cases are the same rule
conflict** — a legible but generic feature at the read-out position with no crossing, which one
clause admits as a 1 and the precedence rule sends to 0. The same boundary produced the human–LLM
kappa gap and a systematic split between the two LLM streams on arithmetic prompts. Three independent
signals, one under-specified rule.

No rating was revised. The criteria were fixed in advance and the ambiguity is reported as a finding
about the rubric, not corrected after the fact.

## Limitations, worst first

**The rating budget is a tenth of what the original work describes.** Anthropic report a few hours per
graph; my median was 262 seconds, and the 5-minute cap was exceeded on 18 of 50 presentations. So the
ratings do not measure whether a mechanism *exists* in a graph — they measure whether one is
*apparent to a calibrated rater within a bounded budget*. That is the quantity a triage rule serves,
but it is narrower than the question the ~25% figure answers and the two are not directly comparable.
The bias runs toward lower ratings, so +0.243 is plausibly a lower bound. Two results are untouched:
the frame-length effect involves no ratings, and the induction cases are legible and circular rather
than incompletely explored.

**The ratings exist for one model and one dictionary.** P5, the audit, the kappa, the rating
distribution and the coverage-without-insight argument are all Gemma-2-2B with GemmaScope per-layer
transcoders. **Nothing in the cross-model work touches them.** Whether the metric tracks human
readability under a cross-layer dictionary, or on another model, is untested — and given how much
else moved, assuming it transfers would be unwarranted. That re-run would need a fresh sample, the
viewer re-pointed at cross-layer features, feature labels that may not exist for that dictionary, and
a rater who has not already seen the per-layer versions.

**Frozen attention.** No QK computation appears in any graph, so the induction result is a property of
frozen-attention attribution and methods that attribute through attention may not share it.

**One rater, not blind to the hypotheses.** Intra-rater consistency is measured; inter-rater
reliability is not. The LLM streams are not a substitute — they applied my rubric, and one helped
write it.

**Coverage is not correctness.** These metrics measure whether a graph covers the computation, not
whether it covers it correctly. The replacement model is an approximation whose perturbation effects
degrade with depth; whether an edge is causal in the real model needs per-graph intervention
experiments, scoped out in advance. **A graph can be well-covered, readable, and wrong.**

**The second model's categories are under-validated.** Code completion, obfuscated text and
syntactic agreement have no validity evidence on Llama, and all three sit inside D3's and D4's
rankings — with code completion driving half the between-category range. Factual recall's pooled
answer rate fails its gate because of one frame format, and **the registered exclusion rule was not
applied to it**, which may flip D4's verdict; the seven-category recomputation is owed.

**Feature labels were truncated in the display** — 48 characters in the text trace, 34 in the diagram.
At least one rating turned on a label the viewer had cut off, which the audit flagged. A presentation
artefact, and an avoidable one.

**n = 40 for everything involving the ratings**, with six graphs in the top bin; n = 8 for every
category-ranking correlation; n = 3 for the dictionary comparison; n = 12 for the format control. No
distinction resting on those should be leaned on.

**Smaller things, recorded rather than smoothed.** The first rating's written reason concludes 0 while
the recorded grade is 1 — a transcription error; recomputing P5 with it as 0 gives +0.217 (p = 0.18),
and the prediction holds either way. Three ratings include interrupted time. An entity-category
criterion was articulated at presentation 48 and applied inconsistently before that. The error rule
does not specify how far down a chain it propagates, and I noted during rating that I had not applied
it consistently. The corruption-ladder analysis pairs rungs by a grouping recovered from row order
rather than an explicit identifier. Pre-run VRAM, RAM and disk estimates for the extension arms were
all wrong — the first by 3× in one direction, the last by 20× — and a disk-quota overrun cost two
arms' raw outputs, which had to be re-run.

## What this means in practice

**Don't use replacement score to choose which prompts to trace, and don't assume what it does on one
model.** On Gemma-2-2B it varies by 0.036 across every prompt type tried, moves 0.032 when you pad a
prompt without changing what it asks, and ranked highest a category a blind rater found uniformly
uninformative. On Llama-3.2-1B it spans 0.142 and category explains 63% of its variance. Both of
those are facts about a model-and-dictionary pairing, not about attribution graphs.

**If you report it per-prompt, report token count beside it.** On this evidence they are not
separable, in any arm.

**Expect dictionary family to matter more than you would guess.** A cross-layer transcoder on the same
model produced a 3.2× stronger length response, 20% shorter paths, and *slightly lower* replacement —
the last contradicting the published comparison.

**The graph can be well-covered and empty.** The most robust case is known/unknown entity: top of the
length-adjusted ranking on both models, rated 0 in four of six cases, never rated 2. The graph shows a
model formatting an answer without showing it retrieving one.

**Measure on the adjacency matrix, not on what the viewer drew.** My own fourfold error came from
parsing displayed edges.

**For anyone building a rating protocol**, the boundary that broke mine is worth knowing in advance: a
legible but generic feature at the read-out position with no cross-position edge. Three independent
measurements found the same ambiguity. An explicit rule for that case is the first thing a second
version needs.

---

## What I would run next

**The rating protocol on cross-layer graphs, or on a second model.** The expensive one and the real
gap. Every human-facing result is Gemma per-layer, and after watching the metric's category
behaviour, length response and category ranking all move between setups, there is no reason to assume
P5 is the exception.

**A Gemma cross-layer arm, to test C5.** Does path length rather than feature density drive the
length effect? Needs ~48 GB of RAM rather than a bigger GPU, so about an hour on a rented box.

**A manipulation that moves feature count and token count independently within a model.** The arms
did this between dictionaries; nothing has done it within one. The corpus spans 583 to 1,649 features
per token, so prompts matched on token count but differing in density could be constructed
deliberately.

**D4 recomputed on the seven gated categories, and a joint test of entropy and top-1 probability.**
Both are minutes of work on already-collected data, and the first may flip a registered verdict.
On the second: their individual coefficients are uninformative at r = −0.958, so the claim that
prompt-confidence measures contribute nothing is currently unsupported in either direction.

**An hour each on ten graphs** — five rated 0, five rated 2 — to measure how many 0s convert under the
budget the original work describes. Ten hours buys a number rather than a caveat.

**A second human rater**, which would turn intra-rater consistency into inter-rater reliability.

The open question I would most like answered by someone else: **is there any cheap property that
predicts readability rather than coverage, and does it survive a change of model?** Nothing I measured
does the first, and almost nothing I measured does the second.

## Reproducing this

Everything ran on free Kaggle notebooks. That was deliberate: the point is a check anyone can apply.

```
Main corpus   google/gemma-2-2b, bf16, GemmaScope per-layer, nnsight backend
              255 graphs, 72 min, 18.1 s/graph, 12.5 GB VRAM peak
Arms          meta-llama/Llama-3.2-1B, bf16, transformerlens backend
              per-layer (mntss/transcoder-Llama-3.2-1B, 17.3 GB)
              cross-layer (mntss/clt-llama-3.2-1b-524k, 20.4 GB, 7.0 GB VRAM peak)
              255 graphs per-layer + 66 per arm on the matched frames
Not feasible  mntss/clt-gemma-2-2b-426k, 28.5 GB -> needs ~48 GB RAM
Noise floor   ~5e-05 on replacement (bf16 re-attribution)
```

Metrics reproduce the library's `compute_graph_scores` to **1.1×10⁻⁶** while also recording the
per-(layer, position) error decomposition, which the library's scalar metrics do not expose.
Influence comes from iterating the Neumann series rather than inverting `(I − A)`: the graph is a DAG,
so the series terminates exactly. The direct/mediated split records each step's contribution
separately and was verified against a hand-computed synthetic DAG.

**The viewer is part of the method, not a convenience** — the rating asks whether a person can read a
graph, so what they were shown matters. Edge selection is top-k first and threshold second: weights
are row-normalised, so a high fan-in node (the logit has thousands of inputs) has tiny weights
throughout and a fixed threshold empties the graph.

### Environment findings worth knowing

**Node layout.** Nodes are `[features | errors | tokens | logits]`, errors layer-major, logits last in
the order of `logit_probabilities`. Locating logits structurally — as nodes with no outgoing edges —
also catches dead features and silently mis-orders the probabilities. That cost a day and one wrong
finding. Take the layout from the graph. Re-validate it per model: layer count changes the arithmetic.

**Structural zeros.** Exactly two kinds of error node carry zero influence: BOS at every layer, and
the last layer at every non-final position. Predicting all 30 from the architecture and matching them
exactly is a cheap check that your indexing is right.

**Error at the final token position is usually the largest, not zero.** I believed the opposite for a
week; it was an artefact of the logit mis-ordering above.

**Measure on the full graph.** The direct-path share parsed from displayed edges was 0.372; computed
exactly it is 0.095.

**The nnsight backend cannot do cross-layer transcoders.** `MissedProviderError` on a cross-layer
decoder, which writes into several downstream layers. Use TransformerLens — and note the two backends
agree to 1×10⁻⁴ on per-layer graphs, so switching is safe for these quantities.

**RAM, not VRAM, is the constraint on TransformerLens.** It holds decoders in CPU RAM and streams
them, so transcoders plus the bf16 model must fit in RAM. A 20.4 GB cross-layer set peaked at 7.0 GB
of VRAM and ran fine; a 28.5 GB one killed a 31.5 GB-RAM kernel outright, with no catchable
exception. And a smaller *model* does not give a smaller transcoder: size goes as features × d_model
× layers, so Qwen-3 0.6B's per-layer set (18.8 GB, 28 layers) is larger than Llama-1B's (17.3 GB).

**`shutil.disk_usage` lies on Kaggle.** It reported 1,145.8 GB free against an enforced ~60 GB session
quota. Check the sidebar, and write results to `/kaggle/working`, not `/kaggle/temp` — the latter is
wiped with the session.

**`prune_graph` dominates runtime** — 115 s of a 172 s per-graph total at four thresholds. A
node-influence threshold curve ranks graphs identically (Spearman 1.000, 1.000, 1.000, 0.994 across 32
graphs) at negligible cost.

**Re-attribution noise** is median 9×10⁻⁵, up to 3.4×10⁻⁴, and does not scale simply with graph size.
Graph structure is fully deterministic; only the bf16 arithmetic wobbles.

**Python 3.13 breaks the install path.** A pinned `huggingface_hub` leaves `diffusers` importing a
symbol it no longer exports, and `nnsight` imports `diffusers` transitively. Uninstall or stub it.

**Watch out for a pandas column named `head`** — it shadows `DataFrame.head()` under attribute access,
and cost me a check that reported 0% agreement where the truth was 18/18.
