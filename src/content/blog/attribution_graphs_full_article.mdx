## Epistemic status

Pre-registered on 16 September 2026, before any corpus data was collected, in an append-only log
with 30+ dated deviation entries. Five predictions were registered; **three failed**. All five are
reported below against their stated thresholds.

What I am confident in: the measurements. 255 of 255 graphs attributed without a single failure,
metrics validated against the library's own implementation to 1.1×10⁻⁶, and a controlled
manipulation whose effect appears in 27 of 27 matched items.

What the reader should discount: anything resting on the 40 human ratings. One rater, not blind to
the hypotheses, a median of 262 seconds per graph against the hours the original work describes, and
criteria developed in discussion with an LLM assistant — which also rated, so agreement between
those streams is not independent convergence.

One model, one size, one dictionary family, bf16 on a free Kaggle T4.

**Two of my own claims are corrected inside this document rather than quietly removed:** an estimate
of the direct-path share that was wrong by roughly fourfold, and a proposed mechanism for the length
effect that my own data contradicts.

## What I found

No usable pre-flight check for attribution-graph quality exists on this model. The search was
pre-registered and the negative result is the headline.

**Replacement score barely moves across prompt types.** Eight categories span 0.036, from 0.709
(arithmetic) to 0.746 (induction). Completeness spans 0.008 and is, for triage purposes, a constant.

**It moves more when you pad the prompt than when you change the task.** Across 27 facts posed in a
short and a long frame, replacement score rose with frame length in 27 of 27 cases (Wilcoxon
p = 1.5×10⁻⁸), by an average of 0.032 — comparable to the entire between-category range. About half
of that is feature count; the corpus cannot resolve the other half, for a reason worth reading.

**Its ranking inverts on one category.** Induction has the highest mean replacement score of the
eight, and every rated induction graph scored 0 — the explanation those graphs give is "the token
appeared earlier", which is complete, well-covered and circular.

**It does not track human readability.** Spearman +0.243 (p = 0.13, n = 40), with mean replacement
non-monotonic in rating: 0.731, 0.729, 0.755 for grades 0, 1 and 2.

**The predictors that carry the signal are about the prompt's shape.** Of the four cheap prompt
properties, token count (+0.018 logit points per token) and a token-frequency proxy (−0.033) reach
significance at
p < 10⁻⁴. Next-token entropy (p = 0.10) and top-1 probability (p = 0.13) do not — but they are
near-collinear at r = −0.958, so neither is individually identified and their separate p-values cannot
be read as "these contribute nothing". A joint test of the two was not run; until it is, their
contribution is undetermined rather than absent.

**What structure exists does not transfer.** Category explains 21–47% of variance depending on
outcome. Leave-one-category-out R² is positive for three of six outcomes, but above 0.07 for only one
— the share of error falling at the read-out position — and that one correlates −0.69 with token
count.

The corpus, the 255 graphs with per-prompt metrics, the ratings with their written reasoning, and
the pre-registration are all released.

## The problem

Circuit tracing via attribution graphs is the leading method for working out how a language model
produced a particular output. Two things are true of it at once.

It produces genuinely new findings — multi-step reasoning, forward planning in poetry, a model with
a hidden goal. And its authors report that it gave satisfying insight on roughly a quarter of the
prompts they tried, at a cost of a few hours of human effort per graph.

So: a high failure rate, expensive attempts, and no way to tell in advance which bucket you are in.
A researcher picks a prompt, spends an afternoon, and three times in four comes away without a
mechanism they can state.

The tooling already computes two quality scores for every graph. Replacement score measures the
fraction of end-to-end influence from input tokens to output that flows through interpretable
features rather than through error nodes. Completeness is a softer, per-node version. The methods
paper notes these are useful "to flag prompts our method performs poorly on" — and then never
develops that use. They are reported as dataset-wide averages for comparing replacement models —
cross-layer transcoders against per-layer transcoders against raw MLP neurons, at matched cost —
never as a per-prompt instrument, and nobody has tested whether they predict a human's success at
reading a particular graph.

That gap is what this project addresses. Two questions:

- Do cheap prompt-side properties — token count, next-token entropy, top-1 probability, token
  frequency, prompt category — predict attribution-graph quality?
- Does replacement score track whether a human can actually extract a mechanism from the graph?

Both answers are useful. A working predictor gives practitioners a check that costs milliseconds
instead of hours. A null result documents that the field's headline quality metric does not support
the use its own authors suggested for it.

## What replacement score measures

This matters for reading the results, so briefly.

An attribution graph is built on a local replacement model: each MLP is swapped for a transcoder,
which re-expresses that MLP's output as a sparse sum of interpretable features; whatever the
transcoder fails to reconstruct is injected as an error node; and attention patterns and
normalisation denominators are frozen at the values they took on this prompt. What remains is linear,
so contributions add and every edge carries an exact coefficient.

The graph then has four kinds of node, and two of them are asymmetric in a way that drives
everything below:

| Node | Incoming | Outgoing |
|---|---|---|
| feature | yes | yes |
| error | **none** | yes |
| token embedding | **none** | yes |
| logit | yes | none |

Influence is computed by pushing mass backwards from the logits one edge at a time. The graph is a
DAG, so the series terminates — and it can only terminate at a node with no inputs. There are
exactly two such kinds: token embeddings and error nodes. Hence **replacement score is the embedding
share.**

One consequence often reached for: a token's embedding enters the residual stream at layer 0 and is
never removed, so the unembedding can read it at layer 25 with no feature in between, and that mass
counts toward replacement while explaining nothing. I measured that path exactly and it is **9.5%**
of the score — real, but small, and progressively diluted by the normalisation denominators as the
residual stream grows. It is **not** why the metric misbehaves; see the correction below.

What matters is the framing. Replacement score measures **coverage** — can this computation be
expressed in features — and coverage is easiest to achieve when there is little computation to cover.
Induction is the clean demonstration.

## The corpus

255 prompts, 8 categories, each grounded in a limitation the methods paper documents qualitatively
but never quantifies.

| Category | n | Grounded in | Validated by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Obfuscated text (4 corruption rungs) | 44 | reconstruction-error blind spot | 11 complete ladders; entropy non-monotonic in corruption, reported |
| Factual recall | 40 | published success case | answers 89% with a `Fact:` prefix on short frames |
| Known vs unknown entity | 32 | inactive-feature / suppression blind spot | round birth years 5/16 vs 16/16, Fisher p = 6.8×10⁻⁵ |
| Multi-hop | 32 | longer chains, error accumulation | answers 94%; cities that are their own state capital removed |
| Code completion | 29 | untested territory | — |
| Two-digit arithmetic | 28 | published success case | correct 96% |
| Syntactic agreement | 26 | short mechanical control | 18/18 by verb-logit margin, no attraction errors |
| Induction / copying | 24 | attention blind spot (QK frozen) | 24/24 strict three-character copying |

Every category claim was checked before any graph was attributed, and two categories were rebuilt
when the check failed. Sentence-frame induction prompts turned out to be solved by frame completion —
the model predicted `ran` or `was` in 20 of 20 — so the category was rebuilt on bigram repetition.

A ninth category, multiple choice, was dropped after two designs failed. Gemma-2-2B does not bind
answers to option letters: the arrow format continued the option list (12/12 predicted `c`, which was
not an option) and the answer format defaulted to `a` 75% of the time. A redesign as forced choice
between two in-context words failed differently — 79% of choices went to the first-listed option,
because repeating the stem created an induction pattern and in-context copying overrode factual
knowledge. Per a rule fixed before the check, it was dropped rather than redesigned a third time.

Prompt length was varied deliberately within category so that it is not confounded between categories
(eta² = 0.131). That decision turned out to matter more than expected.

## The five pre-registered results

| | Prediction | Threshold | Observed | |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **P1** | category ranking predictable from documented failure modes | Spearman, 8 categories | +0.071 (p = 0.87) | **FAILS** |
| **P2** | replacement does not discriminate between categories | range < 0.15 **and** eta² < 0.20 | range 0.036 ✓, eta² 0.238 ✗ | **FAILS** |
| **P3** | error location discriminates where error quantity does not | ≥ 2× | 1.86× | **FAILS** |
| **P4** | completeness even less discriminative than replacement | smaller range | 0.008 vs 0.036 | **HOLDS** |
| **P5** | replacement vs human rating weakly positive | \|rho\| < 0.4 | +0.243 (p = 0.13) | **HOLDS** |

Three failed, and each failed in a direction that sharpens rather than undermines the argument.

**P5 holds, but its threshold was nearly vacuous and I should say so.** The prediction's text was
"positive but weak"; the registered threshold was \|rho\| < 0.4, which an uncorrelated or negative
result would also have satisfied. And +0.243 at p = 0.13 does not establish the positive direction.
So the only thing P5 establishes is that replacement score is not *strongly* related to human
readability. That is still the claim the paper needs, but it is weaker than a passing prediction
sounds. Together with the two falsification rules discussed below, this is the third registered
criterion that tested a bound rather than the quantity the project cared about — which I take to be
the main methodological lesson here: a threshold written as \|x\| < k, with no direction and no
effect-size floor, can be passed by a result that means nothing.

**P2 split.** The range is four times better than it had to be: 0.036 against a ceiling of 0.15.
Replacement score discriminates between prompt types even less than predicted, which is the direction
the prediction wanted. But eta² came in at 0.238, over its own ceiling of 0.20 by 0.038. The two
measures disagree because eta² is a ratio — variance between categories divided by total variance —
and within-category spread is smaller still (standard deviations 0.008 to 0.026), so entity graphs sit
inside 0.024 of each other. Dividing a small number by a smaller one gives a large ratio: category
accounts for about a quarter of the variance in a quantity that barely varies at all. The metric is
statistically structured and practically flat at the same time, and a triage rule needs the second of
those to be false. The substantive prediction is supported; the variance-ratio form of the test is
not. For scale: Anthropic's published gap between per-layer and cross-layer dictionaries is 0.37 vs
0.61 — nearly seven times the entire between-category spread here. The metric distinguishes methods,
not prompts.

**P1 failed informatively.** The predicted ordering was not a guess; it was derived from the failure
modes the methods paper itself documents — frozen QK for induction, reconstruction error for
obfuscated text, suppression for unknown entities. Those limitations predict the metric's own ordering
at rho = 0.07. The two largest errors run in opposite directions: induction was predicted 7th of 8 and
ranks 1st; arithmetic was predicted 3rd and ranks last.

**P3 was the narrowest miss.** Error location explains 44.4% of category variance against
replacement's 23.8% — a ratio of 1.86 against a threshold of 2.0. More interesting than the miss: the
mechanism proposed for P3 was wrong in two of three cases. Obfuscated text was predicted to
concentrate error on corrupted tokens and is third-lowest in concentration; induction was predicted to
concentrate at the copy source and is the least concentrated of all. The most concentrated categories
are entity and multi-hop, both dominated by error at the read-out position.

## Replacement score responds to prompt length, with content held fixed

This was not predicted and is the strongest single result.

Capital-city and state-capital facts from the factual-recall and multi-hop categories were posed in
short, medium and long frames — same fact, same answer, same model, only the wording around it
changing. **27 facts appear in both a short and a long frame**, and 12 of those also in a medium
frame. Across the 27 matched short/long pairs, replacement score rose with frame length in **27 of
27** cases: mean +0.032, median +0.020, Wilcoxon statistic 0 (not a single reversal),
p = 1.5×10⁻⁸. Pairing is at the level of the fact, averaging within frame where a fact appears more
than once.

On the 12 items posed in all three frames the increase is monotonic:

| Frame | Mean replacement |
|---|---|
| short | 0.7147 |
| medium | 0.7378 |
| long | 0.7703 |

Friedman p = 6.1×10⁻⁶.

Two things make this damaging for the triage use. **The effect is as large as the entire
between-category range.** Padding a prompt moves the same fact by 0.032; the eight categories span
0.036. Whatever you do to a prompt without changing what it asks moves the metric about as much as
the difference between asking the model to do arithmetic and asking it to copy a nonce word.

**And it is a controlled manipulation, not a correlation across different prompts.** The
cross-sectional coefficient on token count (+0.0133 per token, p = 3×10⁻¹² on all 255 graphs) could
be explained by longer prompts differing in other ways. The matched pairs cannot: the fact, the
answer and the model are identical. They also give a **larger** estimate — **+0.0458 per token**
within a fact, 3.4× the cross-sectional figure, with R² 0.81 across the 27 pairs. The controlled
number is the one to quote; the cross-sectional one is attenuated by everything else that differs
between prompts.

**A note on units, since several numbers in this section are easy to confuse.** Replacement score is
a proportion, so every *regression* coefficient below is in **logit points per token** — +0.0133
(simple regression, all 255), +0.018 (Day 6's four-predictor model, all 255), +0.0176 (fitted on the
211 non-obfuscated prompts, used as the length-only null for the corruption ladder), and +0.0458
(within matched pairs, on the difference in logits). All four are on the same scale, which is what
makes the 3.4× comparison meaningful; they differ because they come from different models and
different contrasts. The **raw mean differences** — +0.032 across matched pairs, the 0.036
between-category range, the 0.7147 → 0.7703 frame means — are in **probability units**, not logits.
At p ≈ 0.72 one logit point is worth about 0.2 in probability, which is how +0.0458 logit per token
over a mean 4.8 extra tokens gives roughly the observed +0.032.

### What the effect acts through, and why this corpus cannot say

My first guess was structural: each additional token adds an embedding node, which is a terminal for
backward influence, so a longer prompt should collect more mass on embeddings whether or not any
feature was involved. Splitting the score by path length on all 255 graphs says otherwise. Across the
same 27 matched pairs, the direct share falls slightly with length (−0.009, positive in only 11 of 27,
p = 0.05) while mediated replacement — the part that passed through at least one feature — rises in 26
of 27 (+0.035, p = 3.0×10⁻⁸), slightly more than the raw score does. So padding a prompt raises the
*feature-mediated* part of the score, not the residual-stream part.

That leaves a second structural reading, which is the same story as my first guess one node type
over: feature count also scales with token positions, so a longer prompt has more features for
influence to pass through — more *routes*, not more *computation*.

**Roughly half the length coefficient is feature count, and the corpus cannot resolve the rest.**
Across all 255 graphs, replacement rises +0.0133 per token unconditionally; holding total feature
count fixed it rises +0.0068 (p = 0.035), while holding length fixed each additional feature also
raises it (p = 0.015). The two predictors correlate at 0.83 and jointly explain almost nothing more
than either alone — R² 0.195 against 0.176 for length and 0.181 for features, which is the signature
of two near-substitutable regressors. Feature *density* carries independent signal (features per
token, p = 0.006, in the best-fitting model at R² 0.200), and it varies 583–1,649 across the corpus.

**The matched-frame design cannot adjudicate this at all, and the reason is instructive.**
Lengthening a frame adds 4.8 tokens and 4,442 features — about 925 features per token, which is the
corpus mean density of 913. The long frame is not denser, only longer. Tokens and features therefore
move in lockstep within pairs (**r = 0.999**), and a model containing both is pure collinearity: the
token coefficient flips sign and inflates eightfold, from +0.046 to −0.380. That is an artefact of
the collinearity, not a finding, and I am recording it here precisely so that nobody quotes it. The
design that best isolates the length manipulation is the one least able to say what the manipulation
acts through.

So this is not an open question for want of effort. It is unanswerable on this corpus by this design,
and the honest summary is: about half routing, with wide uncertainty, and no way to pin down the
remainder without a manipulation that changes feature count and token count independently.

One confound to note: short frames carry the `Fact:` prefix and long ones do not, so frame length is
entangled with format. The medium frames give a three-point monotonic trend in the same direction,
which makes length the more parsimonious explanation, but the confound is real — and it is resolvable
cheaply, which is the uncomfortable part. Re-attributing the short frames without the prefix is about
seven minutes of GPU time and would settle it. I am reporting it as an open confound rather than as a
limitation of the design, because it is the former.

### The corruption ladder is length too

*Exploratory, and not pre-registered: P1 is a category ranking, and no per-rung test on replacement
score was registered.*

The obfuscation category is the corpus's one dose-response design: 11 base sentences at 4 corruption
rungs each, every comparison within the same sentence. Raw, it looks like corruption raises
replacement score — clean → character-substitution is +0.018, positive in 9 of 11 ladders, Friedman
p = 0.018, Wilcoxon p = 0.0068.

It is length. Corruption fragments text into character-level tokens, so over the same contrast the
prompts gain 10.4 tokens. A length coefficient fitted on the 211 **non**-obfuscated prompts
(+0.0176 logit/token, p = 3×10⁻¹³) predicts a rise of +0.035 — twice what actually happened. Every
rung comes in below its length-only prediction, monotonically: −0.0088, −0.0117, −0.0171.

| Rung | Tokens | Observed | Predicted from length alone | Residual |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| clean | 5.6 | 0.7223 | 0.7223 | — |
| typo | 6.8 | 0.7176 | 0.7264 | −0.0088 |
| random caps | 11.0 | 0.7291 | 0.7408 | −0.0117 |
| char substitution | 16.0 | 0.7403 | 0.7574 | −0.0171 |

Within-ladder fixed effects agree: the rung coefficient flips from +0.0330 (p = 6×10⁻⁵) to −0.0229
once token count enters, while length takes +0.0158 (p = 0.013). The two routes also agree in
magnitude — −0.0229 logit per rung over three rungs is about −0.014 in probability units at p ≈ 0.72,
against the observed −0.0171 residual. The typo rung is the cleanest single case: +1.2 tokens, and
replacement *falls* 0.0047.

So corruption does not raise replacement score; the apparent rise is fragmentation into more tokens,
and conditional on length corruption slightly lowers it. How much is not identified: r(rung, tokens) =
0.897, and the rung coefficient's interval spans zero in both directions. n = 11.

This is the third demonstration of the length effect and the sharpest, because here a deliberately
designed manipulation is entirely absorbed by its own side-effect on token count.

## How much of the metric bypasses the features — a correction

A token's embedding enters the residual stream at layer 0 and is never removed, so the unembedding can
read it at layer 25 with no feature in between. That path counts toward replacement score and explains
nothing. I first estimated its size by parsing the logit's displayed top edges on the 40 rated graphs:
mean share 0.372, correlating +0.56 with replacement score. Recomputing it exactly — from the full
influence vector, splitting replacement by path length, with no thresholding and no top-k — gives a
much smaller number.

| | parsed from displayed edges | computed exactly |
|---|---|---|
| mean direct share | 0.372 | **0.095** (median 0.091, range 0.023–0.184) |
| correlation with replacement | +0.556 | +0.157 Pearson, +0.124 Spearman (p = 0.05) |
| correlation with human rating | +0.033 | −0.089 |

**So the claim that much of the metric bypasses the features is wrong. About a tenth of it does.** The
parsed estimate was inflated roughly fourfold, because the direct edge is nearly always among a
logit's top few inputs while the thousands of small feature paths that carry most of the mass never
appear in a truncated view. I have left the correction visible rather than quietly swapping the
numbers. **The general lesson: any quantity that sums edge weights must come from the adjacency
matrix, because a display threshold is not a random sample of edges.**

What replaces it is more interesting, though weaker than it first looks. Splitting replacement into a
direct part (mass reaching an embedding in one step) and a mediated part (through at least one
feature):

| | Spearman with human rating | mean by rating 0 / 1 / 2 |
|---|---|---|
| replacement score | +0.243 (p = 0.13) | 0.731 / 0.729 / 0.755 |
| mediated replacement | +0.297 (p = 0.06) | 0.656 / 0.664 / 0.672 |
| direct share | −0.089 (p = 0.59) | 0.103 / 0.089 / 0.110 |

Removing the direct component leaves the correlation with readability essentially unchanged — Δρ =
0.054 on n = 40, both p > 0.05 — but monotonic, where raw replacement dips at rating 1. I would not
lean on either half of that. Mediated replacement is close to an affine rescaling of the total (direct
share is 9.5% and varies only 0.023–0.184), so little reranking is arithmetically possible; and the
monotonic trend is three steps of 0.008 against within-category standard deviations of 0.008–0.026,
with only **6 graphs** in the top rating bin. Note also that mediated's spread across ratings (0.016)
is *smaller* than raw's (0.024): monotonic and less discriminative at once.

At category level the reshuffle is larger — code completion rises from 5th to 1st and obfuscated from
7th to 2nd, while induction falls from 1st to 6th and factual recall from 4th to 7th. But that is
reshuffling means which span 0.036 in total, using a component that varies more between graphs than
the categories differ from each other. The sharpest version: P1 re-scored on mediated replacement
gives Spearman −0.524 (p = 0.18, n = 8), against +0.071 on raw. The documented failure modes do not
merely fail to predict the metric — once the interpretable part is isolated, they predict it
substantially backwards. That is exploratory, not a re-test of P1, and eight categories is a small n.

**The honest puzzle this leaves:** the length effect runs entirely through the component I have just
called the interpretable one. Either longer prompts really do more feature-mediated computation, or
"passed through a feature" is no more meaningful than "reached an embedding" — a single generic
formatting feature satisfies it. On the primary outcome this resolves about halfway: holding feature
count fixed, length still predicts replacement (p = 0.035), so routing is part of the story and not
all of it. But the two cannot be separated further on this corpus, for the reason given in the length
section — the frame manipulation moves tokens and features in lockstep at r = 0.999. **The semantic
reading of the direct/mediated split should not be treated as established.**

Replacement scores recomputed in this pass agree with the corpus run to a median of 7.2×10⁻⁵, with
95.1% inside the 3.4×10⁻⁴ re-attribution ceiling; the two exceedances are consistent with a Kaggle
image change between sessions rather than a change in method.

## The inversion

Induction has the highest mean replacement score of the eight categories, at 0.7455. Every rated
induction graph scored 0.

That is not a contradiction, and understanding why is the point of the project.

An induction prompt is `snerrik yolvantz snerrik yolvantz snerrik`. The model predicts `yol` with
p = 0.95. The graph traces the prediction cleanly back to the prompt: two raw token embeddings at the
earlier occurrences of `yol`, a handful of features, almost nothing lost to error. Coverage is
excellent.

And the explanation is: it predicted `yol` because `yol` appeared earlier. Which you knew from reading
the prompt.

The mechanism you actually want — how the model knew to look at that earlier token rather than any
other — is the induction head's query-key computation, matching the current token against its previous
occurrence and copying what followed. That computation was frozen into a constant before attribution
began, so **it is not in these graphs and no amount of looking at them will find it.** Anthropic say
as much about their own version of this case, calling it arguably a regression against earlier methods
that could at least identify induction heads.

**That is a statement about this method as configured, not about attribution graphs in general**, and
the distinction matters because it bounds how far the inversion generalises. Attention is frozen here
because the local replacement model needs linearity; approaches that attribute *through* attention —
QK attribution, and replacement models that decompose attention heads rather than freezing them —
target exactly this failure. If a method recovers the QK computation, induction stops being a category
where coverage is high and insight is nil, and the inversion reported below would be specific to
frozen-attention attribution. The inversion is still the right warning for anyone using
`circuit-tracer` as it ships, which is the method this project set out to characterise, but it should
not be read as a permanent property of the approach.

So the metric's best-scoring category is the one where a human learns least, because high coverage
there reflects a trivial computation rather than a legible one. Arithmetic shows the mirror image:
lowest replacement score, and the graphs explain the model predicting a line break rather than a sum,
because the model does not do the arithmetic.

The full picture across the 40 rated graphs:

| Category | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| factual recall | 0 | 1 | 4 |
| multi-hop | 1 | 2 | 2 |
| code completion | 0 | 3 | 0 |
| syntactic agreement | 1 | 3 | 0 |
| obfuscated | 2 | 3 | 0 |
| entity known/unknown | 4 | 2 | 0 |
| arithmetic | 4 | 3 | 0 |
| induction | 5 | 0 | 0 |

All six top ratings are geographic factual recall or multi-hop — entity → country or state → answer,
the shape of the published Dallas→Austin circuit. Nothing else in the corpus produced one.

Two smaller observations from the same table. Known and unknown entity graphs are indistinguishable by
replacement score (0.742 vs 0.745) and both mostly rate 0: the graph shows the model formatting an
answer without showing it retrieving one, whether or not the person is real. And code completion
produced no 0s and no 2s — every graph a 1 — because the feature labels available for code tokens were
too implausible to name an intermediate. That ceiling is a property of the feature descriptions, not of
the graphs.

## So does a triage rule exist?

No — but **neither pre-registered falsification rule fired**, and the negative headline is therefore a
judgement rather than a verdict.

Section 5 set out two routes to a null. The first: category explains under 10% of variance in every
outcome **and** nothing survives multiple-comparison correction. Category explains 21% to 47%, and 47
of 66 tested terms survive Benjamini–Hochberg. The second: leave-one-category-out R² at or below zero
for **all** outcomes. Three of six are positive. My analysis script evaluated both rules and printed
`NULL RESULT HEADLINE: False`.

For clarity about what the 47 are: six outcomes were each tested against eleven terms — seven category
dummies, each comparing one category against arithmetic as the reference, and the four cheap continuous
predictors. That is 66 questions of the form "does A differ from B" or "does X move with Y", and 47
came back yes. The count is partly an artefact of the reference choice, since arithmetic is the lowest
category on the primary outcome and contrasts against an extreme are easier to detect; and the six
outcomes are strongly correlated with each other, so the effective number of independent tests is well
below 66.

I am reporting the negative conclusion anyway, on three grounds the rules did not encode:

- **The effects are far too small to act on.** The entire between-category range is 0.036, and padding
  a prompt without changing what it asks moves the same fact by 0.032.
- **The one outcome that transfers is prompt length in disguise.** Final-position error share reaches
  leave-one-category-out R² = 0.275 and correlates −0.691 with token count; length alone explains
  47.7% of its variance, more than the fitted model recovers out of category. The share is a ratio
  over positions, so a shorter prompt concentrates error at the read-out position for arithmetic
  rather than mechanistic reasons.
- **The usable model does not survive the honest test.** R² 0.299 in-sample, 0.067 out of category.

The rules were badly specified, and that is worth saying plainly. Both asked whether *any* signal
exists. The project's question is whether the signal is large enough and portable enough to decide
where to spend an afternoon — which Section 6.1 of the same document had already insisted on, in
requiring effect sizes rather than p-values alone. With 255 graphs and a noise floor of 5×10⁻⁵,
"nothing reaches significance" was a bar that could only have been cleared by a broken instrument. What
I should have written was a threshold on the quantities that matter: a null if the best out-of-category
R² falls below about 0.15, or if the between-category range of the primary outcome falls below 0.05.
Both would have fired. I am not retrofitting those as the registered criteria — I am recording that the
ones I registered tested the wrong thing.

The transfer table, which is where the substance is:

| Outcome | Category eta² | Leave-one-category-out R² |
|---|---|---|
| final-position error share | 0.431 | +0.275 |
| replacement score (logit) | 0.238 | +0.067 |
| total error mass | 0.242 | +0.053 |
| completeness (logit) | 0.210 | −0.143 |
| error concentration (Gini) | 0.444 | −0.186 |
| mean path length | 0.470 | −0.535 |

**The ordering inverts.** The outcomes with the most in-sample category structure generalise worst.
Mean path length has the highest eta² and the worst out-of-category R²; its structure is
category-specific in a way four continuous predictors cannot reconstruct for a type they have never
seen.

One more result worth recording, since it points at the only direction I would still look. Cheap
continuous predictors beat the category label in-sample — R² 0.299 versus 0.238 — driven by token count
(+0.018 logit points per token) and the token-frequency proxy (−0.033), both at p < 10⁻⁴. That is the
practically relevant model, because a practitioner has a prompt and not a category. But the same
model's out-of-category R² is 0.067, a fourfold drop, which is the gap the two cross-validation
schemes exist to expose. A model combining category and the continuous predictors is uninformative for
inference: maximum VIF 23.1 against a pre-set threshold of 5, because entropy and top-1 probability
are near-collinear at r = −0.958. Those two reach nothing individually (p = 0.10 and p = 0.13), but
at that correlation each t-test asks only whether one adds anything given the other, so their joint
contribution is **undetermined** rather than absent. A joint test was not run.

## The human rating, and what it cost to do honestly

40 graphs drawn from eight quantile bins of replacement score, 10 re-inserted as unmarked duplicates,
50 presentations in randomised order, rated blind to every metric. Three-point scale: 2 for a causal
path I could state in one sentence naming the intermediate features, 1 for a meaningful feature but no
path, 0 for neither.

The criteria were developed on practice graphs drawn from outside the rated sample and frozen in a file
committed before rating began. Two mechanical checks before awarding a 2: the trace must report at
least one cross-position edge, and a named feature must sit at a non-final token position. A crossing
without an intermediate is routing, not computation; an intermediate without a crossing is the read-out
token elaborating itself.

**What the rater saw.** Not the library's pruned graph viewer — the pre-registration said that, and
D3.11 corrected it before rating began. Each presentation was the backward-reachable subgraph from the
single top-1 logit, selected by recursively taking each node's top four incoming edges to a depth of
four, with a weight floor that only trims beyond the top three. Three properties of that choice bear on
the results. It is **edge-weight selection, not influence-based pruning** — no node threshold was
applied, so the picture is not "the most influential nodes". Because the top three inputs always
survive, **the diagram can display a complete path that carries almost no influence**; the rubric
handles this by excluding magnitude from the rating and flagging weak chains in the written reason, but
"the graph showed a path" is partly a property of the viewer. And a depth of four against a corpus mean
path length of 3.7 hops means **longer graphs were truncated more often** — and path length is the
outcome with the highest category eta² (0.470), so the truncation is unevenly distributed across
categories.

**Reliability.** Intra-rater quadratic weighted kappa on the duplicates: 0.889, 9 of 10 pairs
identical. The single disagreement is traceable — the same graph rated 0 at presentation 20 and 1 at
presentation 45, spanning a criterion I articulated mid-run and recorded at the time.

Two further streams, both exploratory. An LLM rated the same 50 from the stored text with the same
written rubric (kappa with me: 0.68); a second LLM audited my stated reasoning against each graph.
Neither is independent evidence: both were given criteria I wrote, and one of them helped write them.
Agreement measures whether the criteria are articulable, not whether they are shared.

The audit is the part I would keep. Giving a judge the graph, the rubric, my grade and my written
reason, and asking separately whether the reason follows from the graph and whether the grade follows
from the reason, produced a number rating studies rarely report: **12% of ratings were defensible but
rested on an unsupported reason.** The judge disagreed outright on 30%.

And the disagreements were not scattered. Five of the eight flagged cases are the same rule conflict —
a legible but generic feature at the read-out position with no crossing, which one clause admits as a 1
and the precedence rule sends to 0. The same boundary produced the human–LLM kappa gap and a systematic
split between the two LLM streams on arithmetic prompts. Three independent signals, one
under-specified rule.

My own notes caught it too, though less often than a first pass through them suggests. Many reasons
open with "no crossings so either 0 or 1" or "three crosses so could be any rating" — that is the
mechanical check doing its job and narrowing the range before the finer judgement, not hesitation.
Genuine deliberation shows up in about five of the fifty, where "borderline" appears after the evidence
has been weighed. The clearest is a reason that records me resolving to 0 on the error rule, rereading
the criterion, and landing on 1.

I did not revise any rating. The criteria were fixed in advance and the ambiguity is reported as a
finding about the rubric, not corrected after the fact.

## Limitations, worst first

**The rating budget is a tenth of what the original work describes.** Anthropic report a few hours per
graph; my median was 262 seconds, and the 5-minute cap was exceeded on 18 of 50 presentations. So the
ratings do not measure whether a mechanism exists in a graph — they measure whether one is apparent to
a calibrated rater within a bounded budget. That is the quantity a triage rule serves, since triage
decides where to spend the hours, but it is narrower than the question the ~25% figure answers and the
two are not directly comparable. The bias runs toward lower ratings, so +0.243 is plausibly a lower
bound. Two results are untouched: the frame-length effect involves no ratings, and the induction
inversion does not depend on effort, since those graphs are legible and circular rather than
incompletely explored.

**Frozen attention, per-layer transcoders.** Two dictionary-and-method choices bound everything here.
Attention patterns are frozen, so no QK computation appears in any graph — the induction inversion is
a property of frozen-attention attribution, and methods that attribute through attention may not share
it. And cross-layer transcoders score substantially higher (published 0.61 vs
0.37) and produce shorter paths (2.3 vs 3.7 hops) by collapsing chains of near-duplicate features.
Several graphs I rated 1 showed a legible feature embedded in exactly such a chain. Whether the
metric's failure to track readability survives the better dictionary family is the most important open
question this work leaves. The length effect follows from the residual stream and should be
dictionary-independent — though if it runs through the mediated component for the reason I cannot yet
rule out (feature count scaling with positions), a dictionary that mediates differently may behave
differently.

**One rater, not blind to the hypotheses.** Intra-rater consistency is measured; inter-rater
reliability is not. The LLM streams are not a substitute — they applied my rubric, and one helped write
it.

**Coverage is not correctness.** These metrics measure whether a graph covers the computation, not
whether it covers it correctly. The replacement model is an approximation whose perturbation effects
degrade with depth; whether an edge is causal in the real model needs per-graph intervention
experiments, which were scoped out in advance. A graph can be well-covered, readable, and wrong.

**Feature labels were truncated in the display** — 48 characters in the text trace, 34 in the diagram.
This is distinct from the general caveat that Neuronpedia descriptions are automated: at least one
rating turned on a label the viewer had cut off, which the audit flagged. A presentation artefact, and
an avoidable one.

**One model, one size, bf16, free-tier hardware.** Gemma-2-2B with GemmaScope transcoders on a Kaggle
T4.

**n = 40 for everything involving the ratings**, with six graphs in the top bin. No distinction resting
on those six should be leaned on.

**Smaller things, recorded rather than smoothed.** The first rating's written reason concludes 0 while
the recorded grade is 1 — a transcription error; recomputing P5 with it as 0 gives +0.217 (p = 0.18)
against +0.243, and the prediction holds either way. Three ratings include interrupted time. An
entity-category criterion was articulated at presentation 48 and applied inconsistently before that.
The error rule does not specify how far down a chain it propagates, and I noted during rating that I
had not applied it consistently. The corruption-ladder analysis above pairs rungs by a grouping
recovered from row order rather than from an explicit ladder identifier.

## What this means in practice

**Don't use replacement score to choose which prompts to trace.** It varies by 0.036 across every
prompt type tried here, moves by 0.032 when you pad a prompt without changing what it asks, and ranks
highest the category a blind rater found uniformly uninformative. None of that makes it a bad metric —
it was built to compare dictionaries, and it does that well. It was never developed as a per-prompt
instrument, and this is evidence for why.

**Report it per-prompt with length alongside.** If replacement scores are compared across prompts
anywhere, token count belongs in the same table. On the evidence here they are not separable.

**Error location is roughly twice as discriminative** — eta² 0.444 against 0.238 — but it does not
transfer out of category, so it is a better descriptive variable rather than a better triage signal.

**The graph can be well-covered and empty.** The clearest case: a graph whose entire explanation is
that a token appeared earlier in the prompt, traced cleanly, with almost nothing lost to error.
Coverage and insight come apart, and they come apart systematically rather than at random.

**Measure on the adjacency matrix, not on what the viewer drew.** My own fourfold error came from
parsing displayed edges. Display thresholds are not random samples.

**For anyone building a rating protocol for attribution graphs**, the boundary that broke mine is worth
knowing in advance: a legible but generic feature sitting at the read-out position with no
cross-position edge. Three independent measurements found the same ambiguity. An explicit rule for that
case is the first thing a second version needs.

## What I would run next

**The same corpus with cross-layer transcoders.** The single most important check. If the metric tracks
readability once near-duplicate feature chains are collapsed, the negative result here is about the
dictionary family rather than about the metric. Roughly two hours of compute.

**A manipulation that moves feature count and token count independently.** This is what the
routing-vs-computation question needs, and no amount of reanalysis substitutes for it. The frame
ladder adds features at exactly average density, so it cannot separate them; what would is a pair of
prompts matched on token count but differing in feature density — the corpus spans 583 to 1,649
features per token, so such pairs exist in principle and could be constructed deliberately. Until
then, "about half routing" is the ceiling of what can be said.

**A joint test of entropy and top-1 probability.** Also already-collected data, also two lines. Their
individual coefficients are uninformative at r = −0.958, so the claim that prompt-confidence measures
contribute nothing is currently unsupported in either direction.

**The `Fact:` prefix control** — about seven minutes of GPU, as above.

**An hour each on ten graphs** — five rated 0, five rated 2 — to measure how many 0s convert under the
budget the original work describes. That bounds the largest limitation directly, and ten hours buys a
number rather than a caveat.

**A second human rater**, which would turn intra-rater consistency into inter-rater reliability. The
LLM streams cannot do this.

**Whether the length effect holds on another model.** If padding a prompt raises replacement score on
Llama-3.2-1B too, it is structural rather than a Gemma quirk.

The open question I would most like answered by someone else: is there any cheap property that predicts
*readability* rather than *coverage*? Nothing I measured does. The six graphs I could read all had the
same shape — entity at a content position, concept built across layers, carried to the read-out token —
and whether a prompt will produce that shape may simply not be visible from the prompt.

## Reproducing this

Everything runs on a free Kaggle notebook. That was deliberate: the point is a check anyone can apply,
not one that needs a cluster. The full corpus run took 72 minutes, mean 18.1 s per graph, peak 12.5 GB
GPU.

Released: the 255-prompt corpus with cheap predictors and task success, per-graph metrics, the
pre-registration with its full deviation log, the rating manifest and criteria committed before rating,
all three rating streams with their written reasoning, and the viewer used to display the graphs.

### Environment findings worth knowing

These cost most of a day each and are not documented anywhere I could find.

**Node layout.** Nodes are laid out `[features | errors | tokens | logits]`, errors stored layer-major,
logits last in the order of `logit_probabilities`. Locating logits structurally — as nodes with no
outgoing edges — also catches dead features and silently mis-orders the probabilities. That cost me a
day and one wrong published finding. Take the layout from the graph.

**Structural zeros.** Exactly two kinds of error node carry zero influence: BOS at every layer, and the
last layer at every non-final position, since an error injected after the final attention layer has no
path to the read-out token. Predicting all 30 from the architecture and matching them exactly is a cheap
check that your indexing is right.

**Error at the final token position is usually the largest, not zero.** I believed the opposite for a
week; it was an artefact of the logit mis-ordering above.

**Measure on the full graph.** The direct-path share parsed from displayed edges was 0.372; computed
exactly it is 0.095.

**`prune_graph` dominates runtime** — 115 s of a 172 s per-graph total at four thresholds. A
node-influence threshold curve ranks graphs identically (Spearman 1.000, 1.000, 1.000, 0.994 across 32
graphs) at negligible cost.

**Re-attribution noise is larger than a single short prompt suggests:** median 9×10⁻⁵, up to 3.4×10⁻⁴,
and it does not scale simply with graph size. Graph structure is fully deterministic; only the bf16
arithmetic wobbles.

**fp32 does not fit on a T4**, and system RAM rather than VRAM is the binding constraint during load —
use `backend="nnsight"`, which skips the TransformerLens state-dict conversion. The P100 is unusable at
sm_60.

**Python 3.13 breaks the install path.** A pinned `huggingface_hub` satisfies `circuit-tracer` but
leaves `diffusers` importing a symbol it no longer exports, and `nnsight` imports `diffusers`
transitively. Uninstall or stub it.

**Watch out for a pandas column named `head`** — it shadows `DataFrame.head()` under attribute access,
and cost me a check that reported 0% agreement where the true figure was 18/18.
