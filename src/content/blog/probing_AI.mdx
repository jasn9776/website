## Plain text description

AI systems are complex. ChatBots such as ChatGPT, Grok, Gemini, Claude, Deepseek are all large langauge models (LLMs). 
That means they have many layers and are trained on lots and lots of text. However, due to this complexity, it is hard to know what the model is doing inside its layers.
One such behaviour we wish to study is sycophancy, which is exactly what it sounds like: the model is overly tuned to please the user and agree with them. In this study we are 
study a very simple model of only 2B parameters (compared to the &gt; 1 trillion in today's frontier models). We use SAEs and Linear Probes to try to peer into this model and study

## TL;DR

I ran three methods on the same sycophancy contrast — univariate SAE differential analysis (the standard interpretability pipeline), a probe trained on SAE features, and a probe trained on raw residuals — with stratified letter-confound control on Gemma 2 2B. The univariate SAE method identifies 0 features at layer 12 where probes on the same SAE features achieve AUROC 0.75 and probes on raw residuals achieve 0.79: information that the SAE preserves is discarded by the standard analysis method. At layer 20, probe AUROC hits 0.97, but recent work (Boxo et al. 2026; Layer-wise Positional Bias 2026) gives strong reasons to discount this number as inflated by output-commitment leakage rather than treating it as evidence of a clean cognitive representation. The contribution is methodological: any SAE-based behavioural analysis of MCQ contrasts needs at minimum letter-confound control, multivariate probes alongside univariate analysis, and skepticism toward last-token late-layer signal.

## Background

The dominant approach to detecting behavioural patterns in language model activations splits into two methodological camps. Sparse autoencoder (SAE) work decomposes activations into a sparse feature basis and identifies "top features" that differentiate conditions of interest — typically by ranking features on mean activation difference (Panickssery et al. 2024 for CAA-style analysis; Chalnev et al. 2024 for SAE-Targeted Steering; FGAA and SAF for sycophancy specifically). Linear-probe work trains a classifier directly on raw residual stream activations and reports cross-validated accuracy.

A converging consensus from the last 18 months (Kantamneni et al. 2025; the GDM "Negative Results for SAEs" post; Bricken et al. 2024; "Sanity Checks for SAEs" 2026) has been that raw-residual probes match or beat SAE-based methods for downstream classification. This has been interpreted as evidence that SAEs lose information during encoding.

This project asks a more specific question: within the SAE pipeline, where exactly does the information get lost? The standard pipeline does two distinct things — it encodes activations into a sparse basis, then applies univariate analysis to identify discriminating features. I disaggregate these steps by running three methods on the same data with the same confound control, and find that the encoding step preserves nearly all the linearly-accessible information; the univariate analysis is where it disappears.

A second contribution: applying recent findings on probe inflation (Boxo et al. 2026; the leakage literature on Apollo's sandbagging probes) to interpret the late-layer results properly. The 0.97 AUROC I obtain at layer 20 is *not* a strong finding once you understand what late-layer last-token probes are really detecting.

## Methods

**Model and SAEs.** Gemma 2 2B (base), loaded via TransformerLens with `from_pretrained_no_processing` to fit on a free Colab T4. Gemma Scope canonical 16k-width JumpReLU SAEs at residual stream layers 6, 12, and 20.

**Contrast dataset.** The NLP-survey subset of the sycophancy evaluation from Sharma et al. (2023), via Anthropic's `evals` repository. 400 examples sampled with seed 42, prompt length capped at 1024 tokens.

**Behavioural labels.** For each example, I compared the logits of `" A"` and `" B"` at the answer position and assigned `is_sycophantic = True` iff the higher-logit choice matched the dataset's `answer_matching_behavior`. This follows Papadatos & Freedman (2024). Gemma 2 2B behaved sycophantically on 89.25% of the 400 examples.

**Activation extraction.** At the answer-token position, for each prompt, I extracted (a) the raw 2304-dim residual stream activation and (b) the 16384-dim SAE feature encoding, at each of layers 6, 12, 20.

**Letter-confound control.** The naive sample has 93% letter-A in the non-sycophantic group versus 53% in the sycophantic group, so any feature tracking letter looks like a sycophancy feature. I constructed a stratified balanced subsample by matching on the model's predicted letter within each condition. The resulting balanced sample has n=86 with identical A:B proportions across groups. *This is a real cost: 80 of the 86 examples are "predicted A" cases (the matching is bounded by the smallest cell, which has only 3 non-syco-B examples). Generalization to B-preferring examples is essentially untested.*

**Three analyses on the balanced sample:**

1. *Univariate SAE differential expression.* For each SAE feature active on ≥5% of examples in at least one group: two-sided Mann-Whitney U with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction across kept features. Cliff's delta for effect size. Features flagged if q &lt; 0.05 and |δ| &gt; 0.2. This is *more rigorous* than typical published SAE contrastive analyses, which usually rank features by mean activation difference with no significance testing.

2. *Linear probe on SAE features.* Logistic regression (sklearn defaults, L2 penalty C=1.0), 5-fold stratified cross-validation, AUROC as the metric. Input: 16384-dim sparse feature vector.

3. *Linear probe on raw residuals.* Identical setup; input is the 2304-dim raw residual stream.

**Probe robustness checks.** Shuffled-label baselines (target: AUROC ≈ 0.5). Regularization sweep (C ∈ {10, 1, 0.1, 0.01}) to test capacity-dependence of signal.

## Findings

### Without letter control, every method appears to find substantial signal and most of it isn't sycophancy

On the unbalanced N=400 sample, raw-residual probes achieve AUROC 0.74 / 0.82 / 0.93 at layers 6 / 12 / 20. A probe predicting the *letter* (A vs B) — which is not the target behaviour — achieves similar performance at the same layers. The naive univariate SAE analysis identifies many features per layer. None of this is informative until letter is controlled, because the residual stream at the answer position cleanly encodes the model's pending letter output.

### The three methods diverge sharply once letter is controlled

| Layer | Raw-residual probe AUROC | SAE-feature probe AUROC | Univariate SAE features (`q<0.05`, `|δ|>0.2`) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6 | 0.596 ± 0.113 | 0.586 ± 0.199 | 0 |
| 12 | 0.788 ± 0.103 | 0.745 ± 0.121 | 0 |
| 20 | 0.969 ± 0.031 | 0.947 ± 0.033 | 23 |

The probe results are robust to overfitting checks. Shuffled-label baselines give AUROC 0.51 / 0.55 / 0.52 — within noise of chance. A regularization sweep from C=10 to C=0.01 (three orders of magnitude in penalty) shifts the matched-data AUROC at layer 20 by at most 0.005.

### The information is in the SAE basis; univariate analysis is the lossy step

SAE-feature probe AUROC tracks raw-residual probe AUROC within 0.02-0.04 at every layer. The SAE encoder preserves nearly all linearly-accessible behavioural information; it is the univariate analysis applied to that encoding that discards it.

The clearest case is layer 12. The univariate test finds zero features at `q<0.05` and `|δ|>0.2`. A logistic regression on the same SAE features achieves AUROC 0.745. The information that lets the classifier separate the conditions is present in the feature space but distributed across enough features that none individually pass FDR-corrected significance. The standard "top features by mean activation difference" pipeline — which doesn't even include FDR correction — would simply report nothing at this layer.

This is consistent with feature absorption (Chanin et al. 2025) and the broader finding that SAEs do not find canonical units (Leask et al., ICLR 2025): the model's representation of sycophancy at layer 12 is not concentrated in a single SAE feature but spread across many. Whether this distribution is the model's "real" representation or an artifact of how the SAE carved the activation space is a further question — Heap et al.'s FaithfulSAE work (2025) suggests SAEs trained on general data can learn features that don't correspond to model-internal features at all.

### The Layer 20 result is much weaker than it appears

The 0.97 AUROC at layer 20 looks like a strong positive finding. It is not. Three lines of recent work converge on the conclusion that probe performance at the last-token position of a late transformer layer is systematically inflated by mechanisms unrelated to genuine cognitive representation.

First, late layers concentrate residual stream importance on the most recent positions. The Layer-wise Positional Bias paper (Jan 2026) finds that "positional importance increases monotonically toward more recent input positions, with all layers assigning higher importance to tokens closer to the prediction point. This effect strengthens with depth: later layers increasingly concentrate importance on the most recent positions." By layer 20 of 26 in Gemma 2 2B, the residual stream at the answer position is dominated by the pending output decision — what letter is about to be emitted — rather than abstract behavioural state.

Second, and most directly relevant: Boxo et al. ("Linear probes rely on textual evidence," March 2026) evaluated linear probes for sandbagging, sycophancy, and bias under conditions that filter out textual evidence of the target behaviour. They report AUROC reductions of 10-30 points across settings, including a drop from 0.94 to 0.57 for sandbagging on a "model organism" stripped of behavioural verbalization, and 0.74 to 0.57 for bias. Applied to my setup: probes at the answer position are plausibly detecting "I am about to emit the sycophantic letter" rather than "I have an internal sycophancy state." Letter matching partially controls for letter category, but does not remove the broader textual-commitment signal — the residual stream still contains information about which specific letter is being prepared, which tokens will follow, and other surface properties correlating with the labeled behaviour.

Third, the MCQ format itself introduces additional confounds. "Mind the Gap" (2025) finds accuracy differences of up to 11% from tokenization choices around the answer position; "My Answer is C" (Lyu et al. 2024) finds first-token probability evaluation can diverge from text output by 60% or more in instruction-tuned models. Both effects mean "sycophantic at the first-token logit comparison" is not a clean operationalization of cognitive sycophancy.

The honest interpretation of the layer 20 result is: there is a near-linear direction in activation space at the answer position that predicts the model's pending letter output and its categorisation as sycophantic. This is consistent with the model having a cognitive representation of sycophancy, but it is also consistent with the probe detecting the textual commitment to a specific output, which is downstream of whatever process produced the sycophantic decision. The current data does not distinguish these.

The layer 12 result (0.79 / 0.75 / 0) is more defensible because it sits before the output-commitment region of the network, in the regime where the leakage literature suggests probes are more likely to be detecting representational state rather than imminent output.

## What this means

**Univariate SAE analysis is the wrong default for behavioural detection.** The standard pipeline: rank SAE features by mean activation difference, optionally apply significance testing, report top features — is well-suited to concentrated representations and badly-suited to distributed ones. For sycophancy in Gemma 2 2B at the answer position, the representation is distributed across enough features that the univariate approach reports null findings where the actual signal is substantial. A negative result from univariate SAE analysis is uninformative about whether the model represents the concept.

**The "probes beat SAEs" finding is more precisely "probes beat *single-feature analyses applied to* SAEs."** Once you train a multivariate classifier on SAE features, the gap to raw-residual probes nearly disappears (0.04 AUROC). The recent papers showing probes outperform SAEs (Kantamneni et al. 2025; GDM 2025) do partially address this — k-sparse probes with k>1 perform better than k=1 — but the standard SAE-evaluation methodology that drives published "feature discovery" work continues to focus on individual features. The corrective is not to abandon SAEs but to pair them with multivariate analysis.

**MCQ contrasts are systematically confounded by letter and by output-commitment effects.** Letter confounds inflate apparent feature counts by an order of magnitude. Output-commitment effects inflate late-layer last-token probe AUROCs by 10-30 points. Neither is visible without explicit controls. Any contrastive analysis on MCQ-format safety benchmarks should at minimum: match on letter or adjust statistically; report results at multiple positions, not just the answer token; treat late-layer last-token AUROC > 0.9 as a flag for likely leakage rather than evidence of cognitive representation.

## Limitations

The substantive limitations of this work:

- **Model scale.** Gemma 2 2B is small. Linear-probe and SAE work consistently shows representations are more legible at larger scales and with instruction-tuning. The specific finding that sycophancy is distributed rather than concentrated may not replicate on larger or instruction-tuned models, where representations tend to become more concentrated and univariate analysis may work fine.
- **Asymmetric matched sample.** Letter matching yielded n=86, of which 80 are "predicted A" cases. Generalization of the layer-12 and layer-20 findings to B-preferring examples is essentially untested. A direction trained on A-preferring examples might or might not transfer to B-preferring ones; with only 6 B examples we cannot say.
- **No cross-distribution generalization test.** The strongest critique of activation-based probes (Goldowsky-Dill et al. 2025; Boxo et al. 2026) is the leakage finding: probes may detect textual evidence rather than cognitive state. The standard test is train-on-one-distribution, evaluate-on-another. I attempted a philpapers replication but it was complicated by a different MCQ schema (up to 7 answer options) that the 2-option matching strategy doesn't handle cleanly. The high probe AUROCs reported here are vulnerable to the leakage critique and would need cross-distribution validation before being interpreted as evidence about Gemma 2 2B's internal representation of sycophancy.
- **One position.** I sampled only the answer-token position. The sycophantic computation may happen earlier; reading only the answer position misses the upstream representation.
- **No causal validation.** Whether ablating the probe direction or any of the 23 univariate-significant SAE features would change Gemma 2 2B's sycophantic behaviour is untested. The natural follow-up is activation patching or feature ablation.
- **Single contrast.** "Sycophantic vs not" is one of several behavioural concepts where this comparison could be run. The distributed-vs-concentrated representation question may have different answers for refusal, deception, harmful content, or other behaviours.

## Related work and where this sits

The methodology I applied has direct precedents:

- **Probes outperforming SAEs.** Kantamneni et al. ("Are Sparse Autoencoders Useful?" Febƒ 2025); the GDM Mech Interp Team's "Negative Results for SAEs" (March 2025); Bricken et al. ("Using SAEs for harmful intent detection" Anthropic 2024); the "Sanity Checks for SAEs" paper (Feb 2026).
- **Probe leakage on textual evidence.** Boxo et al. ("Linear probes rely on textual evidence" March 2026) — the closest precedent for my layer-20 caveat, tested specifically on sycophancy among other behaviours.
- **Multiple-testing in SAE feature discovery.** "Which Sparse Autoencoder Features Are Real? Model-X Knockoffs for FDR Control" (Nov 2025) is the first paper I am aware of to apply formal FDR control to SAE feature significance testing. My use of Benjamini-Hochberg correction follows similar logic, applied earlier in the pipeline.
- **Letter / position confounds in MCQ evaluation.** "Mind the Gap" (2025) on MCQ tokenization effects; "My Answer is C" (Lyu et al. 2024) on first-token-vs-text-output divergence.

What I think is somewhat novel: the explicit three-way comparison disaggregating SAE encoding from univariate analysis, with letter-confound control applied uniformly across all three methods. This isolates the failure mode (univariate analysis discards distributed signal) more precisely than the typical "probes vs SAEs" framing. I haven't seen this specific disaggregation in published work, but I'd welcome pointers if it exists.

What I think is not novel: the underlying finding that SAE-based approaches lose to probes is well-established. My contribution is to explain *which step* of the SAE pipeline is responsible.
