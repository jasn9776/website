## The short version

- There is a popular method for explaining why a language model produced a particular word. It
  works maybe a quarter of the time and costs hours per attempt. Nobody knows in advance which
  quarter.
- The tooling already computes a quality score for each attempt. I tested whether that score, or
  anything cheap about the prompt, predicts success.
- **It doesn't.** On the model I tested first, the score barely varies between wildly different
  kinds of prompt, and the thing it varies with most is how *long* the prompt is — not how
  interesting it is.
- **Then it got more interesting.** I ran the same test on a second model, and most of my findings
  changed. The score's behaviour turns out to be a property of the specific model-and-toolkit
  pairing, not of the method.
- That second result is the useful one. A quality score you'd have to re-calibrate for every new
  setup costs more to validate than the work it was meant to save.

I registered 15 predictions in advance, before collecting any data. **Eight of them failed.** All
15 are reported below.

## What this is about

### Attribution graphs

When a language model answers a question, you can try to trace *why*. One leading method builds an
**attribution graph**: a diagram that follows the output backwards through the network to the input,
showing which internal components carried the signal.

The components aren't raw neurons — they're **features**, which are more interpretable. A feature
might correspond to something like "this text is about a capital city" or "an answer is expected
here." So a good attribution graph reads like a chain: *this word activated a "Texas" feature,
which activated an "Austin" feature, which produced the answer.*

This method has produced real discoveries — multi-step reasoning, a model planning ahead while
writing poetry, a model concealing a goal.

### The problem

The researchers who built it report that it gave them satisfying insight on roughly **a quarter**
of the prompts they tried, at a cost of a few hours of human effort each.

So: pick a prompt, spend an afternoon, and three times in four you come away with nothing you can
state. There was no way to tell beforehand which it would be.

### The opening

The tooling already computes a score for every graph, called the **replacement score**.

Here's what it means. To build the graph, the method replaces each messy layer of the network with
a set of readable features. Those features can't capture everything, so the leftovers get bundled
into "error" nodes — honest placeholders for *we couldn't express this part*. The replacement score
is the fraction of the traced influence that ran through readable features rather than through the
leftovers. A score of 0.75 means three-quarters of the explanation went through things that have
names.

The method's authors note this score could be useful "to flag prompts our method performs poorly
on" — then never develop it. It's reported as a single average per dataset, to compare toolkits.
Nobody had tested it per-prompt, as a way to decide where to spend an afternoon.

### Three questions

1. Do cheap, easily measured properties of a prompt predict whether its graph will be worth reading?
2. Does the replacement score track whether a human can actually extract a mechanism from the graph?
3. Does any answer to 1 and 2 survive changing the model?

## What I did

**255 prompts in 8 categories**, each chosen to probe a weakness the method's authors describe but
never quantify: obfuscated text, factual recall, well-known vs obscure people, multi-step questions,
code, arithmetic, grammar agreement, and copying patterns.

Every category was validated before any graph was built — does the model actually do the task this
category is named for? Two categories were rebuilt when the check failed, and one was dropped
entirely after two failed designs.

**Then I built a graph for every prompt, on two different models**, and measured each one. About 700
graphs in total, including follow-up runs. Plus a human rating exercise: 40 graphs, rated blind,
scored 0 to 2 for whether a mechanism could be stated in one sentence.

All of it on free cloud compute, which was deliberate — the point is a check anyone can run.

---

## What's confirmed

These either survived a controlled manipulation or replicated across models.

### 1. The score responds to prompt length, not just content

This is the strongest result in the project.

I asked the same factual question in a short frame and a long frame — same fact, same answer, same
model, only the padding around it changed. **In 27 out of 27 pairs, the longer version scored
higher.** Not one reversal.

The effect is as large as the entire difference between the eight prompt categories. Padding a
prompt moves its score by about as much as switching from arithmetic to code.

The direction held in every model and toolkit I tried.

### 2. It's not because longer prompts have more features

My first explanation was mechanical: more words means more internal features, so more routes for
the signal to travel. More *paths*, not more *thinking*.

I tested this across three different toolkits, holding the length change fixed at about 4.8 added
words. If the effect were really about feature count, adjusting for features added should make the
three agree.

**It made them disagree more** — the spread between setups went from 3.5× to 6.3×. And the toolkit
with the *fewest* features per word had the *strongest* effect, which is backwards.

I had previously estimated that about half the length effect was feature count. That estimate is
withdrawn. My own later data killed it.

### 3. A graph can be highly readable and tell you nothing

The clearest case: prompts about well-known versus obscure people.

These score at the very top once you adjust for length — on **both** models. And a blind rater
scored them 0 ("no mechanism I can state") in four of six cases, with **not one** scored 2.

The graphs show the model *formatting* an answer without showing it *retrieving* one. Whether the
person is real or invented makes no difference to the score.

So coverage and insight come apart. The score measures whether the computation can be *expressed*
in readable features — and that's easiest when there's barely any computation to express.

### 4. The second quality score is worse than the first

The tooling also computes a "completeness" score. It discriminates between prompt types even less
than the replacement score does — on both models. One of the few predictions that held cleanly
twice.

### 5. The measurements themselves are solid

Worth stating separately, because every criticism below is about *interpretation*, not arithmetic:

- 255 graphs on each model, **zero failures** either time
- my metrics match the official library's to 7 decimal places
- two completely different computational backends agree to 4 decimal places on the same prompts
- re-running the same graph gives answers that differ in the 5th decimal place

### 6. The numbers change a lot when the model changes

Running the same 255 prompts on a second model moved nearly everything:

| | Model A (Gemma-2-2B) | Model B (Llama-3.2-1B) |
|---|---|---|
| spread of scores across prompt types | 0.036 | **0.142** |
| share of variance explained by prompt type | 24% | **63%** |
| rank of copying prompts (of 8) | 1st | **5th** |
| strength of the length effect | strong | **5× weaker** |

On the first model the score barely distinguished prompt types. On the second it distinguishes them
quite clearly. **That these two setups disagree is firmly established.** What it means is discussed
under "not confirmed" below.

## What's not confirmed

Things that may be true, that I could not establish.

### Whether the score predicts what a human gets out of a graph

This was question 2, and it's the one that mattered most.

The correlation between replacement score and my blind ratings was **+0.24, not statistically
significant** at 40 graphs. Worse, the threshold I'd registered in advance ("absolute value below
0.4") would have been satisfied by a result of zero, or by a negative one. It tested the wrong
thing.

And this exists for **one model and one toolkit only**. Nothing in the second-model work touches it.
Given how much else moved between models, assuming it carries over would be unwarranted.

### Whether the second model's clearer signal is real

The score's bigger spread on model B rests heavily on one category — code. Remove code prompts and
the spread falls from 0.142 to 0.063, and the variance explained from 63% to 40%.

Code prompts score far lower on model B (0.60) than on model A (0.74), where they were unremarkable.
That's consistent with a plainer explanation: **model B's toolkit may simply have a gap where code
is concerned** — a dictionary that can't express code tokens well. That would be a fact about
toolkit training coverage, not about prompt types.

I read through model B's actual code completions informally and they look like reasonable code. But
no rated check against fixed criteria was run, so **this category remains unvalidated on model B**,
along with two others. The question stays open.

### Whether "it depends on the model" is a general rule

Two models is two data points. That these two differ is established. That the score's behaviour is
*generally* model-dependent is a reasonable inference from two points, not a demonstrated law.

The rank agreement between the two models' orderings is +0.48 — moderate, and not statistically
significant at eight categories.

### Whether documented weaknesses predict anything

The method's authors describe specific blind spots. I predicted an ordering of categories from them.

On model A the prediction correlated **+0.07** with reality — essentially nothing. On model B, **+0.57**
— quite well. Neither is statistically significant at eight categories. The two point estimates are
far apart and the test can't tell them apart from each other or from zero.

### What the length effect actually works through

Feature count is ruled out (above). The best remaining candidate is how many steps the signal
takes to reach the input — shorter chains, stronger effect — which held across all three toolkits.

But that's three data points, and I noticed the pattern after seeing the data. So I registered it
as a prediction for a future run rather than reporting it as a finding. That run needs about 48 GB
of memory and hasn't happened.

### Everything about the newer toolkit type at scale

There are two families of these toolkits. I only ran the full 255-prompt corpus with the older
kind. The newer kind was tested on 66 prompts — enough to show the length effect survives, not
enough for anything about categories.

## What's ruled out

Things I believed, that the data killed.

**"Much of the score bypasses the readable features entirely."** A word's representation enters the
network at the start and is never removed, so the output can read it directly with no feature in
between — influence that counts toward the score while explaining nothing. I estimated this at 37%.

Computed properly, it's **9.5%**. My estimate came from reading the graph's *displayed* top
connections, and the display shows only a handful of the strongest links while thousands of small
ones carry most of the weight. A 4× error, entirely self-inflicted.

**"Longer prompts score higher because they have more input nodes."** No — splitting the score by
path length shows the *direct* portion slightly *falls* with length while the feature-mediated
portion rises in 26 of 27 pairs. Padding raises the interpretable part.

**"Corrupting text raises the score."** It looks like it does. But corruption shatters words into
character fragments, so the prompt gets longer. Once you account for length, corruption slightly
*lowers* the score. A designed experiment entirely swallowed by its own side effect on word count.

**"Errors concentrate where the documented blind spots predict."** Obfuscated text was predicted to
concentrate error on corrupted words; it's third-*lowest*. Copying prompts were predicted to
concentrate it at the copy source; they're the *least* concentrated of all eight.

## What this means if you use these tools

**Don't use the replacement score to choose which prompts to investigate.** On the first model it
varies by 0.036 across every prompt type tried, moves by 0.032 when you pad a prompt without
changing what it asks, and rates highest a category a blind rater found uniformly useless.

**Don't assume what it does on one model.** On the second model it behaves quite differently. Both
of those are facts about a model-and-toolkit pairing, not about the method.

**If you report it per-prompt, report word count next to it.** On this evidence they can't be
separated in any setup I tested.

**Expect the toolkit to matter more than you'd guess.** Swapping toolkit families on the *same*
model changed the length effect by 3.2× and contradicted the published comparison between them.

**Measure on the underlying numbers, not on what the viewer draws.** That's where my 4× error came
from.

## What I got wrong

Listed because a project that only reports its successes isn't worth reading.

- **A fabricated claim.** An early draft asserted that obfuscating text *raises* the score, citing
  a per-rung analysis that was never run. The number quoted belonged to a different test entirely.
  Caught on direct challenge, removed.
- **The 4× direct-path error** described above.
- **A mechanism retracted.** I proposed feature count as the route for the length effect, then my
  own later data refuted it.
- **An overstated superlative.** I reported copying prompts as scoring highest of all eight
  categories. True as stated — but they're also the longest prompts, and adjusting for that drops
  them from 1st to 3rd. Part of the lead was the length effect this project itself discovered.
- **Two registered falsification rules that tested the wrong thing.** Both asked whether *any*
  signal exists. With 255 prompts and a very low noise floor, "nothing reaches significance" was a
  bar only a broken instrument could clear. The project's actual question was whether the signal is
  *large enough to act on*.
- **A rule I wrote and then didn't follow.** I registered, in advance, that any category where the
  model fails the task more than 30% of the time gets excluded from two of the tests. One category
  came in at 32.5%. I included it anyway. Correcting this might flip one verdict; the recomputation
  is still owed.
- **Three wrong feasibility estimates.** Graphics memory overstated by 3×, main memory never checked
  at all though it was the binding constraint, and disk space misread by 20× — which triggered a
  reset that destroyed two runs' output.

## Limits worth knowing

**The rating budget was a tenth of the original work's.** The method's authors describe hours per
graph; my median was 262 seconds. So the ratings measure whether a mechanism is *apparent to a
calibrated rater on a budget*, which is narrower than whether one is *there*. The bias runs toward
lower ratings.

**One rater, not blind to the hypotheses.** I measured my own consistency (0.89 agreement on
hidden duplicates, 9 of 10 identical) but there's no second human. Two AI assistants also rated,
but using criteria I wrote and one helped write, so their agreement measures whether the criteria
are *articulable*, not whether they're *correct*.

**The audit is the part I'd keep.** Giving an independent judge the graph, my grade and my written
reason, and asking whether each follows from the last, found that **12% of my ratings were
defensible but rested on an unsupported reason**, and the judge disagreed outright with 30%. Five of
eight disagreements were the same ambiguous rule. I changed no ratings — the criteria were fixed in
advance, so the ambiguity is reported as a finding about the rubric.

**Coverage isn't correctness.** These scores measure whether a graph *covers* the computation, not
whether it covers it *correctly*. A graph can be well-covered, readable, and wrong.

**Small numbers throughout.** 40 graphs for anything involving ratings, with only 6 in the top
scoring bin. 8 for every category-ranking correlation. 3 for the toolkit comparison. 2 for
everything about model dependence.

**The second model's categories are under-tested.** Three of eight have no validity evidence on it,
and one of those drives half the headline difference.

## What I'd do next

In order of value:

1. **Run the rating exercise on a second model.** Every human-facing result is from one model and
   one toolkit, and after watching everything else move between setups there's no reason to think
   this one is the exception. The expensive one, and the real gap.
2. **Settle the code question.** Run the 29 code prompts through the second model's *other* toolkit.
   Same model, different toolkit — that isolates the variable. Roughly 29 graphs.
3. **Test the path-length explanation** for the length effect. Needs more memory, not more time.
4. **Build prompts that vary feature count and word count independently.** Nothing has done this
   within a single model.
5. **Add a second human rater**, turning self-consistency into actual reliability.

The question I'd most like someone else to answer: **is there any cheap property that predicts how
readable a graph is, rather than how well-covered it is — and does it survive changing the model?**
Nothing I measured does the first. Almost nothing I measured does the second.
