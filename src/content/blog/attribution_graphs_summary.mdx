# When should you not trust an attribution graph? — one-page summary

**Jason Soo** · November 2026
Full version: `writeup.md` · Plain-language version: `writeup_plain.md`

**The question.** Attribution graphs are the leading method for explaining why a language model
produced a particular output. Their authors report satisfying insight on roughly a quarter of
attempts, at a cost of hours each, with no way to tell in advance which quarter. The tooling already
computes a per-graph quality score — **replacement score**, the share of traced influence running
through interpretable features rather than through unexplained error nodes. Does it, or anything
cheap about the prompt, predict whether a graph is worth reading?

**What I did.** 255 prompts in 8 categories, each probing a weakness the method's authors document
but never quantify; every category validated against model behaviour before any graph was built, two
rebuilt when the check failed and one dropped. Attributed on **Gemma-2-2B**, again on
**Llama-3.2-1B**, plus three matched-frame dictionary arms — about 700 graphs, **zero failures** in
either 255-prompt corpus. 40 graphs rated blind for whether a mechanism could be stated in one
sentence. Pre-registered before data collection: **15 predictions, 8 failed.** Free-tier compute
throughout.

### Result 1 — there is no usable pre-flight check

On Gemma, replacement score spans **0.036** across all eight prompt categories, while a
content-preserving manipulation — padding the frame around the *same fact* — moves it **0.032** in
**27 of 27** matched pairs (p = 1.5×10⁻⁸). Out-of-category predictive R² is **0.067**, and the one
outcome that does transfer (+0.275) correlates **−0.69** with token count: it is prompt length in
disguise. Correlation with human ratings: **+0.243**, not significant at n = 40.

### Result 2 — and almost none of it transfers

| | Gemma-2-2B | Llama-3.2-1B |
|---|---|---|
| between-category range | 0.036 | **0.142** |
| variance explained by category | 24% | **63%** |
| rank of induction prompts (of 8) | 1st | **5th** |
| within-pair length slope | +0.0458 | **+0.0087** |

So "statistically structured but practically flat" is a property of **one model-and-dictionary
pairing**, not of the method. A practitioner told "the metric is flat, don't triage on it" would be
wrong on Llama. This is the stronger argument against per-prompt triage, because it does not depend
on the effects being small: a score requiring re-validation per setup costs more than the tracing it
was meant to triage.

### What replicates — directions, not magnitudes

- **Replacement score rises with prompt length**, content held fixed, in every setup tested; the
  magnitude varies 3.5×, the direction never flips.
- **Not explained by feature count.** Across three dictionaries at a fixed +4.78-token manipulation,
  normalising by features added makes the spread *worse* (3.51× → 6.31×), and the lowest-density
  dictionary has the strongest effect.
- **Coverage and insight come apart.** Known/unknown entity prompts top the length-adjusted ranking
  on **both** models, were rated 0 in four of six cases and never rated 2. The graph shows a model
  formatting an answer without showing it retrieving one.
- **Completeness is the weaker of the two scores** on both models.
- **The measurements hold:** metrics match the library to 1.1×10⁻⁶, two attribution backends agree to
  1×10⁻⁴ on the same prompts.

### Ruled out

Direct residual-stream influence bypassing all features is **9.5%** of the score, not the 37% I first
estimated from the graph viewer's displayed edges — a fourfold self-inflicted error, and the reason
any edge-summing quantity must come from the adjacency matrix. Corruption does not raise replacement;
the apparent rise is fragmentation into more tokens, and conditional on length corruption slightly
lowers it. Documented blind spots do not predict where error concentrates: obfuscated text is
third-*lowest*, induction the *least* concentrated of eight.

### Not established

Whether the score tracks human readability anywhere — all 40 ratings are Gemma per-layer, one
non-blind rater, a median of 262 s against the hours the original work describes. Whether Llama's
sharper signal is real: `code_completion` carries half of it and is unvalidated on that model.
Whether "it depends on the model" is a law rather than two data points (rank agreement +0.48, ns).
What the length effect acts through — feature count is refuted, path length is a three-point hunch,
registered as a prediction and unrun.

### Corrections made in place

An early draft asserted that obfuscation *raises* the score, citing a test never run; caught on
challenge and removed. A proposed mechanism for the length effect was retracted when later data
refuted it. Induction's reported superlative is partly length — rank 1 raw, rank 3 adjusted. Two
registered falsification rules tested whether *any* signal exists rather than whether it is large
enough to act on. A validity gate I registered was not applied to the one category that failed it;
correcting that may flip one verdict, and the recomputation is owed.

### Practical upshot

Don't triage on replacement score, and don't assume what it does on one model. If you report it
per-prompt, report token count beside it — on this evidence they cannot be separated. Expect
dictionary family to matter more than you would guess: a cross-layer dictionary on the same model
gave a 3.2× stronger length response and *lower* replacement, contradicting the published
comparison.

The open question: **is there any cheap property that predicts how readable a graph is rather than
how well-covered it is, and does it survive a change of model?** Nothing measured here does the
first; almost nothing does the second.
